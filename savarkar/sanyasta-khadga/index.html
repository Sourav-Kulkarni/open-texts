    Sanyasta Khadga

    Act I

    Scene 1

    (The temple of the Goddess in the hermitage. Dancing girls sing a song.)

    Song:

    Song Details:
    Title: Kitī tari sulalit sahaj-gīti
    Rāga: Yaman | Tāla: Ektāl | Chāl: Hālā, Madhumatī Vilasit

    Original:
    किती तरि सुललित सहज- गीति- । तार न बहु ताणी ।। ध्रु. ।।
    ताणिशि तनु-बीन तुटे शिथिल तरी गीति नुठे, अति ते करित हानी ॥ १ ॥

    Translation:
    How graceful and easy the melody – yet strain not the string too much, (Chorus)
    Shouldst thou strain the lute-string of the body, it snaps; should it be slack, the melody rises not, causing great harm.

    (As the girls depart singing, Siddhartha Gautama and Kaunḍinya enter.)

    Siddhartha: Kaunḍinya, that was the song! Didst thou hear it? This song which these dancers sang just now, I heard it once before. And thou wouldst be astonished, but this song of the dancers kindled a divine light in my heart, like unto a mystic mantra. And in that light, I could trace the path to nirvāṇa (liberation) through the deep darkness of this hermitage and reach the foot of the Bodhivṛkṣa (Bodhi Tree). It is well-known that we were engaged in severe self-mortification on the banks of the Nirañjanā river, to witness firsthand what truth lay in the path of tapas (asceticism), which declares that liberation is attained by torturing, tormenting, and destroying one's body and senses, offering them as a sacrifice. Kaunḍinya, is there any means to free this entire world from the four great sorrows: birth, death, old age, and sickness? With such a yearning, I vowed that—

    Song:

    Song Details:
    Title: Vimukta karu he
    Tāla: Tritāl | Chāl: Pītanake

    Original:
    विमुक्त करु हे । आर्त जगत । पासुनि जन्ममरणा ।। ध्रु. ।।
    साधन शोधाया । राज्य स्वजन अरे प्राणही । सोडीन लोक शोक हरणा ।। १ ।।

    Translation:
    This sorrowing world shall we free / From birth and death's decree. (Chorus)
    To seek the means / My kingdom, kin, yea, even life / Will I forsake, to end human grief.

    Siddhartha: ...With such a resolute determination, I renounced this sorrowful worldly existence, abandoning my Śākya kingdom, my young wife Goddess Yaśodharā, my newborn son Rāhul – all my possessions – and came to dwell in this forest. Then, I observed many vows of fasting and austerity, even to eating but a single grain a day. Yet, enlightenment eluded me. Finally, I lay day and night, nigh unto death. But this only destroyed what little power of thought I had; how then could greater knowledge be attained? Just then, one day, the dancing girls passed by, singing this very song: ‘Do not loosen the string of the vīṇā (lute) too much; else no melody shall rise. Nor stretch it too taut, lest the vīṇā itself break.’ The same condition applies to the senses, this lute of the body. If these senses are excessively indulged, thou shalt miss the music of peace; if thou torturest them too much, thou shalt also forfeit the music of peace. But if thou nurturest these horses of the senses appropriately, yet holdest their reins like an expert charioteer, and makest use of them, then that state of equipoise shall render thy journey on the path of knowledge pleasant, and ultimately grant thee nirvāṇa. This principle convinced me, and I abandoned that self-mortification. I partook of food and, maintaining a balanced diet and conduct, was able to experience the supreme bliss of nirvāṇa through the path of contemplative knowledge under the shade of that Bodhivṛkṣa. Kaunḍinya, is the principle concerning the sacrificial path of violence likewise true for this path of self-mortifying tapas? Tell me, pray! What is the principle of the Tathāgata (one who has thus come/gone) Buddha regarding the sacrificial path of violence?

    Kaunḍinya: O Bhagavan Buddha-deva, I have fully grasped thy principle regarding violent sacrifices. Plavā hyete adṛḍhāḥ yajñarūpāḥ (These forms of sacrifice are unstable boats). Gather heaps of cow dung and firewood, make a huge bonfire of it, and then, with solemn mantras, slice and dice the throats, hearts, fat, and sinews of innocent, frolicking lambs and calves, their legs bound, as one would cut vegetables; and then, with streams of blood, burn their various pieces of flesh in the fire, saying, ‘This is for Indra, this for the Maruts, this for Varuṇa!’ If deities are appeased by such bloody sacrifices, why should they not be appeased by a slaughterhouse? This is not ritual; this is butchery, and if gods are truly pleased by it, then they must be demons, not gods! They say that by slaying lambs and calves, man attains liberation! What madness is this! Moreover, even in the heaven attained by sacrifice, the tales of the gods themselves tell us that they too are not free from envy, wrath, fear, sorrow, and discontent. But, O Bhagavan, did those who devised the notion that liberation is attained and ancestral spirits are appeased by animal sacrifice, invent it solely to deceive the world?

    Siddhartha: Nay. I do not say that they intentionally deceived the world. It was an honest error on their part. It was but a step in humanity's commendable efforts to discover the eternal and immortal fundamental principle of the world and to find a means to freedom from all suffering. It was an intellectual delusion. But once that illusion is dispelled by experience and reflection, what wise man would cling to it still? By self-mortifying tapas, one might, at best, learn what transformations the human organs and powers can undergo through various experiments; but they bear no direct relation to freedom from all suffering and the attainment of the soul's eternal peace. Violent sacrifice or self-mortifying tapas is not the path to nirvāṇa. The path to nirvāṇa means the destruction of craving, and the means are simply these, according to one's conviction:

    A kuśalasyākaraṇe kuśalasyopasaṁpadā |
    Sacittapariyādapana etad Buddhānuśāsanam ||
    (To cease from evil, to cultivate good, to purify one's mind — this is the teaching of the Buddhas.)

    Kaunḍinya: In this hermitage, wert thou not to show me the wondrous forms of self-mortification?

    Siddhartha: Come, enter within! (The curtain opens.) Siddhartha: Behold these types of self-mortification for every occasion, from the hairs of the head to the nails of the feet! See these Keśī (Hair-keepers). They consider it a sin to remove even a single hair. See their hairy bodies, as if draped in an old mat, with hair from the head reaching their backs and faces, from the nose to their moustaches, from their moustaches to their beards, and from their beards to their stomachs, and from their armpits to their ribs!

    Kaunḍinya: But who is this, angrily tearing out his matted locks? Has some grievous news reached him, perhaps?

    Siddhartha: Nay. He is an adherent of the Keśaluñcaka (Hair-pluckers) sect, quite opposite to the Keśī sect. They consider it a sin to leave even a single hair on the body. Moreover, since hair removed by a kṣurikā (knife) would not constitute true self-mortification, they first grow their hair long, then tear it out hair by hair with handfuls, plucking each one out.

    Kaunḍinya: Who are these, raising their hands as if calling someone?

    Siddhartha: That later. First, behold these, who deem it meritorious to keep their arms hanging down their whole lives, just as they were born. They never raise their arms. As a result, their bones wither, and their arms cannot move forward, backward, or upward. Opposite to them, behold these true ascetics. Their belief is that true tapas means continually holding their arms aloft against nature. By keeping both arms constantly raised, they have shrivelled like wood. How can one say which of these mutually opposing paths truly yields merit?

    Kaunḍinya: But who are these, licking the ground?

    Siddhartha: These are performing the Mṛgavrata (Deer-vow). They walk always on all fours, like animals. They sleep and sit like them and subsist on grass and leaves from the ground. Behold these Agatika (Motionless ones). They sit perpetually in one place. In the rain, mud accumulates around them, forming anthills, in which snakes take residence. They survive if someone puts food in their mouths; sometimes snakes show them affection, sometimes they bite and kill them. Behold these Satata Gatika (Constantly Moving ones), quite opposite to the Agatika. They are constantly in motion. If they stay in one place, they will spin around and around; but they will not cease movement. They become accustomed to sleeping while walking forward, or walking even in sleep. Ascetics observing completely opposing rules consider their respective rules with equal stubbornness to be meritorious and leading to liberation. Now, if the vow of motion is meritorious, then the vow of motionlessness must be sinful; and if motionlessness is meritorious, then constant motion is sinful! One of them is certainly mistaken, and most likely, both are mistaken; the joyful thing is only that these greatly restrained ascetics perform their chosen observances side by side, without opposing seekers of an entirely opposite conviction. Kaunḍinya, how many kinds of vows shall I tell thee? Fifty types merely concerning food. Some, to conquer the sense of taste, squeeze extremely bitter juices into every food they eat; some are Nirāśana (Fasters), eating nothing at all; while others are Sarvāśana (Omnivorous). They consider it meritorious to eat whatever appears before them when hungry, even if it be excrement, which they devour with equanimity. Some constantly sit amidst five fires, while others remain perpetually in cold water. See this one, renowned as the most prominent among these self-mortifying ascetics. He has driven sharp nails into the bottom of his sandals and plunges his feet onto their points as he walks. He purifies his eyes by igniting a fumigation of chillies and suspending himself. He burned his genitals on a heated griddle; and now, finding these pains of self-mortification insufficient, that extremely austere ascetic is eating thorns and will perpetually lie upon a bed bristling with spikes.

    Kaunḍinya: Enough, Kaunḍinya! Enough! O Bodhisattva Siddhartha, I have no need whatsoever for further information about these dreadful sects!

    Siddhartha: They say that if a man commits sin in this world, he must suffer such dreadful punishments in hell. But these innocent people, mistaking the infernal stone of sin for merit, voluntarily endure those torments here on earth. And they consider suffering such hellish agonies to be an easy path to heaven, to freedom, to liberation. Behold, this is mere intellectual delusion! Kaunḍinya, did thy mother love thee? Was she compassionate?

    Kaunḍinya: Sire, she was most affectionate. A mother, indeed! How can I describe her love?

    Siddhartha: Then would she rejoice if thou wert hungry, ate grass, or burned thy hand in the hearth; or would her wish be that thou shouldst eat thy fill, be robust and content?

    Kaunḍinya: Assuredly, that I should be well-fed, joyful, and healthy –

    Siddhartha: Well, Kaunḍinya, these folk consider God to be compassionate and the parent of His subjects, do they not? Is it then possible that such a compassionate parent would be pleased by starving His devotee to emaciation or burning him on a heated griddle? A god who is more pleased the more a living being is tormented must be a demon, not a god, must he not? Perform these acts for wondrous siddhis (supernatural powers); but to observe these dreadful vows and fasts, believing that a compassionate God is pleased by self-mortification or sacrificial violence and that liberation is thereby attained, is sheer folly. To me, who set forth to alleviate the world's suffering, to forcibly plunge the world into the suffering of these dreadful vows seems but cruelty.

    A Disciple: (entering) — The Saṅgha (community of monks) is prepared to depart, according to the command of the Tathāgata Buddha to journey to Kapilavastu, his birthplace.

    Siddhartha: Very well. Kaunḍinya, come, our aged father, King Śuddhodana, has wasted away and become utterly frail since we left the kingdom without his knowledge and went to the forest. Yielding to his summons to visit him at least once before his imminent demise, we have resolved to visit our birthplace, the capital of the Śākya nation. Moreover, there is a possibility that this personal opportunity may serve a greater global purpose. We shall strive to the utmost to crown with success our ardent desire to eliminate warfare, violence, strife, and discord from this world, and to establish everywhere a righteous kingdom of dharma (righteous duty), by imparting the teaching of the Four Noble Truths we discovered to the Śākya nation, just as the Kings of Kosala and Magadha became followers of the Tathāgata Buddha, and by making the royal family and military commanders of the Śākya nation our adherents. Kaunḍinya, if all mankind comprehends that there is no suffering like worldly existence, then why would wars and conflicts arise for the illusory gains of this exceedingly painful world? Just as no wise man contends to be bitten by a venomous serpent, so no living creature will contend to leap into the pyre of this worldly existence. Kaunḍinya, truly, there is no suffering like worldly craving. There is no happiness like dispassionate renunciation. Behold—look at that distant mountain, see how the forest fire blazes!

    Song:

    Song Details:
    Title: To vaṇavā bagh dūrī
    Chāl: Tū Ānabān Velī

    Original:
    तो वणवा बघ दूरी । जीव जंतु जाळी । भडकत दशदिशा । आग ।। ध्रु. ।।
    पेटत पळ पळ तैसा । तृष्णाग्नीत । संसार जळत हाय ॥ १ ॥

    Translation:
    Behold that distant forest fire / It burns all creatures, / The flames rage in all directions. (Chorus)
    Like that, moment by moment, / This worldly existence burns / In the fire of craving, alas!

    [End of Scene 1]

    Scene 2

    Kshara is seated. Just then, Shankambhat enters.

    Shankambhat: Kshara, again you have come onto the osari (verandah) and seated yourself with such abandon! All and sundry will come onto the osari; the osari is a public market place, and there you sit. Now, if I were to combine these two words and call you by that name, you would claim I abuse you! The proper place for women to sit is the saipakghar (kitchen).

    Kshara: Silence, you chagalkutle (babbler)! If I am not to sit upon the osari, then what shall I do? You say women's place is in the saipakghar, but what accursed difference is there between a saipakghar and an osari when not a single grain of food have you ever procured to cook in that very saipakghar! If you, like other industrious men, were to make the rupees clink and jingle upon the osari, then I too would be busied with domestic chores in the saipakghar like other settled women. But what accursed stone has God bound about my neck! My life has been squandered. Else, I would have graced the throne as queen to some king.

    Song:

    Song Details:
    Title: Joda Mam Kiti Vijod
    Chāl: Ari Rang Range Gyan
    Original: जोडा मम किती विजोड । हे ठब्बु मी छान । मी गाय । हे गिधाड । हे बदसुर । मी तान ।। ध्रु. ।। गूळचि मी चव ज्याला । त्याचि न हे तेचि जाण । मज पूर्ण मज समचि । पति दे न भगवान् ।। १ ।।
    Translation: How mismatched is my pair! He, a dullard; I, comely. I, a song; he, a vulture. He, off-key; I, the melody. (Chorus) I am the jaggery that has taste, But its flavour, he perceives not. To me, one wholly like myself, A husband, God does not grant.

    Shankambhat: What is it you say, Kshara! Are you in your senses! Do you truly believe you ought to have obtained a husband exactly like you (‘tujasamachi pati’), a perfectly suitable husband? Foolish woman! If that were so, you would weep. If your husband were exactly like you, then though called a man, he would be but a woman! Those who bless maidens with 'may you obtain a suitable groom (‘anurupa varapraptiraastu’)' know not the calamity (anartha) implicit in their words. Nay, there is a slight, inherent mismatch by birth between women and men, and therefore their marital bond is formed. For this reason, give thanks to God that your husband is thus somewhat mismatched with you, not entirely suited to you. Were it otherwise, Kshara, you would be weeping, verily weeping.

    Kshara: And now what else have I but my divinely ordained weeping! Great was your father’s laukik (reputation) as a priest. But you have brought it to dust with your extravagance and idleness. The custom (vritti) of rich citizens has vanished, yet these few rustic folk still come to our door; I thought we might earn a few coins by telling them some sankalp bikalp (ritual intentions and such)! Shall I bid them enter then? They have made two rounds already. In such a state, with naught to eat nor to scratch for in the house, it is not seemly to only perform the duties of a priest when a wealthy patron (yajaman) is present.

    Shankambhat: What you say is quite right. This is not a question of wealth. This is primarily a question of shraddha (faith). Shraddha does not depend on wealth, but on how much of his wealth one is prepared to donate, to offer as dakshina (honorarium). Many rich folk begrudge even offering a betel nut; whereas many an impoverished soul, even at the cost of his very life, will not fail to give dakshina with cupped hands full. Thus, the measure of a man's religious shraddha is not how much dakshina he can give, but how much dakshina he desires to give—how much he desires to give in cash. Go, measure their shraddha by that scale! Bring in the faithful one who has brought the most dakshina. Drive away the other faithless ones. Kshara, go, tell them this is the command of your husband, Shankambhat.

    Kshara: But how can one succeed by focusing solely on dakshina! What if someone has no dakshina—

    Shankambhat: Then it should be understood that he lacks shraddha.

    Kshara: But are not these ten patrons coming to our door because we are mendicant priests (bhikshuk)? Yet—

    Shankambhat: Enough of your 'buts' and 'however's! You say ten patrons come to the door! Women should call but one man their master. I alone am patron enough. Go. Wait! Look, the one who gives the most dakshina must also be ashikshit (unlettered). Just as you dislike a pearl heavier than your nose, so I do not favour a patron more learned than the priest. Go!

    Ksharabai exits and brings in an ignorant farmer.

    Farmer: I bow at your feet, O divine one.

    Shankambhat: Blessings (Kalyan). (To Kshara) You, go inside now. I said go, did I not! I do not approve of women standing thus upon the osari before another man.

    Kshara: Here, I go, once and for all!

    Shankambhat: (Aside) He appears to be a rustic indeed. (Aloud) Is it a teerthvidhi (pilgrimage ritual) you wish to perform, or a shraddha (ancestral ritual), or a snansankalp (ritualistic bath vow)! Why do you stumble so? These Sanskrit names for rituals, you, a rustic, may not comprehend, so I shall ask you in the common tongue (prakrit). Listen! How many rupees have you brought as dakshina? Forty, or thirty, or twenty?

    Farmer: (Aside) Ah, so? I must teach him a little common parlance by seizing this chance. (Aloud) O divine one, I have brought five rupees as dakshina.

    Shankambhat: Then in all of Sanskrit literature, there is no religious rite (dharmavidhi) by that name. Ten rupees are the minimum required!

    Farmer: Very well, O divine one, I shall manage to give that much somehow.

    Shankambhat: Good. That ritual which in prakrit is called 'ten rupees', in Sanskrit it is called a snansankalp. Come, be prepared for the pilgrimage vow (teerthsankalp)!

    Farmer: But one must go to a pilgrimage site (teerth), no?

    Shankambhat: Foolish fellow. Every priest's house is itself a teerth! If I sprinkle water with sacred grass (darbha), you gain the merit (punya) of an ordinary teerth. If I sprinkle it with my finger, you gain the punya of bathing directly in the Bhagirathi. Come, you recite the vow (sankalp). I shall sprinkle the water. Then you may go to the river and take dips all day, or never bathe again in your entire life. You will assuredly gain the punya of bathing. Come. Sit cross-legged. Take out your dakshina, count it, place it here; join your hands. Whatever word I tell you, pronounce it clearly. When I make hand gestures, you must make them too, mind you! Speak, what is your name? Hmm. Om Namah—

    Farmer: Speak, what is your name? Hmm. Om Namah!

    Shankambhat: No, no. Say what your name is, then say Om Namah!

    Farmer: No, no. Say what your name is, then say Om Namah!

    Shankambhat: Your name first; then Om Namah.

    Farmer: Your name first; then Om Namah.

    Shankambhat: Curse you! Just Om Namah!

    Farmer: Curse you! Just Om Namah!

    Shankambhat: What a donkey you are!

    Farmer: What a donkey you are!

    Shankambhat: You paji (rascal)!

    Farmer: You paji!

    Shankambhat: Your father is a paji.

    Farmer: Your father is a paji.

    Shankambhat: (Picking up the dakshina) Thief!

    Farmer: (Snatching the dakshina) Thief!

    Shankambhat: Take that! (He delivers a punch)

    Farmer: Take that! (Delivers a return punch)

    Finally, they come to blows. The Farmer seizes Shankambhat by the ear, forcing his neck down and bending him.

    Shankambhat: Ayayayi! Oh, run to my aid! Oh Mother, oh Mother!

    Kshara peeks through the doorway and runs away. Shankambhat: Oh, come back, why do you go! Oh Mother!

    Kshara: You are calling out to your Mother, are you not? Then your Mother shall descend from heaven; you said again, you do not like women to come onto the osari.

    Shankambhat: Oh, I do like it—Good heavens! Oh! Not Mother, you come! You, Kshara, O Madam Ksharabai, O Madam Ksharabai Maharaj! Come quickly!

    Kshara: (Entering) O Farmer-gentleman, release him! Forgive whatever he may have said, for my sake. His tongue is a trifle fatkal (sharp-tongued). Have mercy!

    The Farmer releases his ear. Shankambhat rushes headlong into the house. The Farmer bows to Ksharabai and departs.

    Shankambhat: (Taking an old sword, peeking cautiously from behind the doorway) Oh, is he gone?

    Kshara smiles and nods, indicating 'he's gone', whereupon Shankambhat emerges and speaks loudly. Shankambhat: Is he gone indeed! Where did the coward (bhyad) flee? What shall I do! Had he been here now, I would have torn him to pieces, verily pieces! Why did you release him? I had given my ear into his hand to test you! You believed it true and said before my enemy that I was fatkal of tongue! And now you have bid my enemy flee, granting him life (pranadan). I understand your deceit (kapat). Kshara, did you speak with another man (parpurusha)? A husband is the king of his wife! You have betrayed him (droh)! Therefore, according to the strictures of treason (rajvidroha chya nirbandhanusar), I shall sever your head! You said my tongue is fatkal, no; my sword is more fatkal than my tongue. Speak, why did you intervene? What if that coward were to come here now! Coward, come forth, let me see!—

    Farmer: (Re-entering, rebuking) What?

    Shankambhat: (Hiding the sword behind his back and discarding it, he retreats behind Kshara and nudges her) I thought he had gone far away! Oh, intervene, intervene, appease him!

    Kshara: I shall not appease him! Women should not even stand and speak with another man; to intervene in their affairs is out of the question.

    Shankambhat: When commanded by one's husband, there is no harm in standing and speaking with another man, nay, even in intervening. Appease him! Seeing his eyes, whirling like a chakra, Kshara, my head spins and I feel dizzy.

    Kshara: Good man, you may go now! Seeing his pitiful state, you should feel amusement rather than anger. Go now, good sir!

    The Farmer bows and departs; just then, Baan enters.

    Shankambhat: Who? Baan! Was it not your guard upon the ramparts of Prince Siddhartha’s pleasure-palace, he who is now called Buddha? What an unfortunate man, that Siddhartha, this Buddha of today. He preferred to roll in the ashes of a cremation ground rather than upon a bed of flowers with beauties, each more exquisite than Yashodhara!

    Baan: He found it agreeable; but his abandoned wife, Devi Yashodhara, is weeping bitterly. Her plight is truly pitiful.

    Shankambhat: Oh, hear you not, how wretched is the plight of women when abandoned by their husbands! Therefore, I tell you, cherish me, else—

    Kshara: What, else what? Because her husband was a great, valiant prince, Yashodhara was heartbroken when he abandoned her for the forest. But women upon whose neck such a burden (khes) falls, they, on the contrary, feel like making vows, making vows, that their husbands should at least have the sense to go into exile! Yes, if a husband whose companionship is truly joyous accepts exile, then it is sorrow! But as for husbands by whose fault the domestic life of women like me becomes as arduous as exile—why do they not go into exile today, if they are to go tomorrow? Will you go now?

    Shankambhat: I would have gone; but as you say, since our domestic life is itself exile, what distinction remains in exile now! There are wild boars in the forest, and (pointing at Kshara) are there none here, pray?

    Kshara: (Pointing at Shankambhat) —Indeed there are! Who but a wild boar would perform such an act as you have just done, burying your snout in the mud of slander against a hero of renunciation (tyagaveer) like Siddhartha, this Buddha?

    Shankambhat: Tyagaveer! Oh, pray tell! What unprecedented renunciation (apurva tyag) did Siddhartha perform that you now call him a tyagaveer! After indulging in all pleasures from birth until satiation, anyone would grow disgusted. After experiencing the passionate delights of youth, day and night for thirty years, in the cool embraces of numerous moon-faced beauties (chandranana), each more exquisite than Yashodhara, that he should then feel a slight chill and wish to warm himself by a forest fire for a while—what wonder is there in that, and what heroism (veeratva)? Let alone thirty years; but if anyone were to appoint me commander-in-chief of the host of beauties in such a pleasure-palace for at least thirty days, on condition of then departing for exile, I would sign that agreement right now! Let me revel in such a pleasure-palace for thirty days, and as its month's rent, I shall happily walk into the forest on the thirty-first day. But how shall our desire for a home be fulfilled? The lot that has fallen to us is such a character! What wonder then that our unsatiated ghosts of sensual pleasures should still hover around the domestic sphere? And oh, oh, just because Siddhartha, this Buddha, abandoned Yashodhara and left, it is not as if he has abandoned the world entirely! His Highness has merely gone off somewhere for a couple of days to taste piquant, astringent, and sour chutneys, having grown weary of the rich delicacies of royal pleasures. He will certainly return! If he returns as a king, then the royal harem (ranivasa) is here; if he returns as an ascetic (bairagi), then they will make her adopt the garb of an ascetic (bairagini) herself. It is all one and the same. Oh, oh, men cannot but keep women (kamini), these jewels among women (striratne), on their persons, under one pretext or another. Be he a householder, or one living in seclusion in a forest retreat, without women (kaminiwachun), they may last perhaps four days; but on the fifth day, back they come for penance (prayashchitta)! Be they one who chants prayers (japi), one who performs penance (tapi), one with a topknot (shikhi), or a renunciate (sannyasi); some will keep a beloved wife, others a beloved disciple (priya shishya)! Some as a lover (anuragini), others as a female ascetic (bairagini)! Ramachandra was a slave to Janaki's lotus-face, and Hanuman to her lotus-feet—but all told, slaves to women (lalnanche das)! Therefore, do not be in such haste to create such a fuss (stom majavun) about Siddhartha Gautama’s exile and deride us householders. If this Buddha does not return for Yashodhara, today or tomorrow, then my name is not Shankambhat!

    Kshara: Silence! You have a habit of slandering any great man you see.

    Shankambhat: Silence! You have a habit of calling anyone I slander great.

    Kshara: Today, even in the heat of slandering Siddhartha, you have at least acknowledged that men are slaves to women, have you not?

    Shankambhat: Yes, to women; but not to such a slave-woman as that!

    Baan: Reverend Priest! I have some urgent business (nikadiche kaam). You know that I am a sentinel on the ramparts around the pleasure-palace. The day Siddhartha fled, I am accused of leaving the palace gates open. For years, I have bought time by one means or another, but now time will surely claim me. They say the King will inflict capital punishment (prandand) upon me. No counsel comes to mind.

    Kshara: So you came to him to seek counsel, did you?

    Baan: No, Madam, do not be angry! How can I seek the wrong thing in the wrong place; but I heard the reputation that the Reverend Priest gives tait (amulet) consecrated with mantras to ward off calamities, so I came.

    Shankambhat: An tait for the calamity of capital punishment, eh? At least five tola (tola - a unit of weight) of gold are required?

    Baan: B-b-b-b! Will three tolas not suffice?

    Shankambhat: My good man, is the body you wish to save made of three great elements? The body is made of five great elements (panchamahabhuta), so five tolas of gold! Come, produce the money—

    Baan: I refer you to Seth Sunas.

    Shankambhat: Seth Sunas? We are their hereditary priests (pidhijata upadhyaya). Excellent. Well, now you must say that a rumour (gapp) has spread in the village that some wondrous (adbhut) things happened on the night Siddhartha fled; do you know of any such adbhut occurrence?

    Baan: Yes, indeed, one adbhut thing did truly happen. That night, before the rampart gates were closed, while I stood guard, I felt like sitting, then like sleeping, and then fell into a deep slumber! But how can I recount this adbhut thing?

    Shankambhat: (Laughing) Bravo, Baana, bravo! In short, you dozed off and slept on duty—is that not the adbhut thing? Well, did anything else adbhut occur?

    Baan: Nothing else happened!

    Shankambhat: It did.

    Baan: It did not, Maharaj!

    Shankambhat: Baana, do you wish to live or to die?

    Baan: To live, Maharaj.

    Shankambhat: Then say it happened. An adbhut thing hap-pened.

    Baan: It happened. An adbhut thing happened. But which one?

    Shankambhat: It is this: that while you were on guard, you closed the gates. You secured the bolts, locks, latches, and hooks with a clatter. But just then, an angel came and passed his hand over your eyes and said, "Be unconscious!" And you all fell unconscious, and upon waking at dawn, found the doors still firmly shut. You remember nothing of what transpired in between; you must say this by rote! My tait will do the rest.

    Baan: But—

    Shankambhat: Silence. This is precisely what you must say. Oh, you fool, to ward off the public scandal (lokapavad) of these naive, faithful folk, the shield of divine miracle proves useful. The maiden Kunti (upavar Kunti) bore a child before marriage. Public scandal arose! In reality, there was no exception in it. It is merely a natural law. But when it was seen that lokapavad concerning the illegitimate son (kanin putra) was inevitable, Vyasa cleverly threw in the tale of the divine miracle of the Sun-mantra, and the foolish doubts were satisfied by a cunning answer. So too shall it be today. Not only this, but if in time Siddhartha Gautama truly becomes a great founder of a religion, then even in the new Puranas of his followers, this fanciful tale (bhakad katha) I have suggested will attain great glory as a religious story (dharmakatha). Though my name may not shine in that Purana like Vyasa's, who is to say it will not float upon the narrative stream and land upon the shores of future generations! Well, come inside, I will give you the tait.

    Vikramsingh: Why has the chaughada sounded but once, then ceased? Has Siddhartha been sighted from the city's perimeter?

    Messenger: Sighted, you ask? Your Majesty, he has already entered the city!

    Shuddhodan: Then why have not the ten directions yet resounded with welcoming instruments and acclamations for my son? What a grave error!

    Messenger: Forgive me, Your Majesty! But the chaughada at the perimeter gate, which was to give the first signal, was silenced by Siddhartha himself as soon as the first beat fell, thus confusing the people at the city gates. Each time the chaughadas attempted to resume, Siddhartha had them silenced again. Meanwhile, leaving the main gateway, Prince Siddhartha entered the city by a side path; avoiding the designated and adorned route for his procession, he deliberately proceeded through the squalid, narrow, and outcast lanes where the humble, poor, and untouchables reside, soliciting and accepting alms. And an immense multitude stands frozen along the main thoroughfare, gazing at the sight with astonishment!

    Shuddhodan: What? My son, the heir of King Shuddhodan, the Crown Prince of this Shakya nation, wandering through his own capital, begging for alms at the doors of untouchables? Has this thorn brought disgrace upon my entire lineage? Born into a Kshatriya (warrior class) royal clan, yet begs for alms! He takes food from those whose very shadow is considered Vitāḷ (defilement/pollution)! Oh, messenger, how dared those untouchables offer him alms? How felt they no fear of my wrath?

    Messenger: Your Majesty, to Buddhadeva—

    Shuddhodan: Who is this Buddha? What Buddha? What sort of Buddha? He is Siddhartha Gautama. Therefore, at least in my presence, he must be called Siddhartha.

    Messenger: My apologies! None but Your Majesty alone possesses the power to deter Siddhartha from this contact with the food of untouchables.

    Shuddhodan: Chief Minister, bring my horse at once! This impropriety must be halted by me! Though I have grown old, I am still the King of the Shakyas. This rebellious conduct of Siddhartha, which stains the honour and creed of that proud nation and lineage, must be punished by me! Bring the horse, go! Go! And command the people to clear the path. Else, I shall not hesitate to charge my horse headlong through the throng.

I felt that every idle emerald within this crown was a drop of the sweat of those farmers and labourers, and every greedy ruby and jewel within this crown was a drop of the soldiers' blood. A king's thirst is like a rakshasi (demoness), drinking the blood of these toiling labourers. And this crown is the cup from which that demonic royal thirst drinks blood. O Crown! A kamandalu filled with drops of pure water, equal to the number of jewels within you, and a cupped palm full of grains of rice, are more useful than you. For these may be drunk by the lowly and thirsty, and eaten by the hungry. But you, most useless, shining pebbles, which cannot be drunk, nor eaten – begone! Return to the rubbish heap from which foolish men gathered you, deeming you jewels! (He attempts to cast away the crown, but the royal attendants catch it mid-air.)

    Scene 7

    (Buddha, his disciples, and Commander Vikram are seated.)

    Buddha: Commander Vikramdev, you are the chief of this Shākya Rāshtra (Shākya Nation). You are also a great sage among the Shākyas, and I know that in your youth you attained perfection by meditating on the profound truths of philosophy with a disciplined mind, observing the discipline of yajña (ritual sacrifice), charity, and austerities. Therefore, one of my main objectives in returning to Kapilavastu is to reveal to you, especially among all the Shākya people, the treasury of ārya satya (noble truths) I have discovered, and to secure your cooperation in carrying the momentum of this great dharmachakra pravartan (turning the wheel of dharma) to the ends of the earth, thereby freeing all beings from sorrow. It is my desire, therefore, that you accept the sanyāsa (renunciation) initiation and join this bhikṣusaṅgha (monastic order). Alas, this very world, even heaven, burns in the fire of tṛṣṇā (craving). The wise man must cast it off like vomit. Just as a creature escaped from a forest fire does not re-enter it, and no one desires a disease to return once cured, so too the wise man does not delight in this world again. In such a state, I fail to comprehend how perfected beings such as yourself can remain in this world, and especially in this military profession, so tainted by violence and treachery.

    Vikram: How so, Lord? Just as you, the Ender of Sorrows, can remain in this body afflicted by birth, sickness, old age, and death, even after attaining Nirvāṇa (liberation/extinction of desire)! I ask the Tathāgata (epithet for Buddha): Does this physical body, along with that wisdom and the cessation of tṛṣṇā upon the attainment of Nirvāṇa, instantly dissolve, vanish, melt away like a mirage? If not, is it desirable to forcibly destroy it, to commit suicide, to instantly break the shackles of the body? If not, must not even the liberated, even the Buddhas, sustain their bodies? Even if it be videha-mukti (bodiless liberation), if physically it is sadeha (with body) until death, then must not the mundane duties of bodily sustenance be performed? Moreover, great souls like yourself do not wander alone in the forest like a rhinoceros, indulging in the joy of liberation, but rather, moved by the sorrows of the afflicted, you establish this vast enterprise of preaching, propagation, the Saṅgha (Buddhist monastic community) of disciples, debates, monasteries, and abbacy, all to bestow upon the people the benefit of eternal happiness. Why is that?

    Buddha: Commander, it is true that the body does not vanish immediately after the attainment of Nirvāṇa. If so, you ask: 'Is it desirable to forcibly abandon the body after Nirvāṇa?' To that, the Tathāgata's clear answer is that forcible abandonment of the body is a new karma (action/deed), and as such, it leads to rebirth. And to transcend birth and death is indeed the Tathāgata's goal. Therefore, the Tathāgata has not abandoned his body. The bodily existence, a consequence of past karma, will cease of its own accord when its time comes! Far from destroying the body, even self-mortification is not sanctioned by the Tathāgata. The Middle Path is the golden path. Keeping the body healthy and efficient and constantly wearing it out in altruistic service for the welfare of the people, this is true bodily austerity. Compassion is the beloved of the liberated, and constantly showering the nectar of Nirvāṇa upon those afflicted by sorrow, motivated by that compassion — this is the renunciant's worldly existence.

    Vikram: Then renunciants, too, have a worldly existence! You mentioned the doctrines of rebirth, karma, and the fruits of karma, as other-worldly principles, as reasons for not destroying the body immediately upon liberation. I shall set aside discussion of these as irrelevant to my present purpose. And I desire to discuss only the worldly aims, nature, and consequences of the practical conduct of living out one's bodily existence, or the renunciant's worldly existence inspired by altruism, such as great souls like yourself establish—since even those who have themselves attained Nirvāṇa, who have experienced self-realization and self-grace, deem immediate bodily destruction undesirable. I ask the Tathāgata: Is not the main difference, in your opinion, between the conduct of a liberated one living in a monastery – concerning food, clothing, and sleep – and that of an bound householder, simply that the former acts dispassionately, for subsistence, or for egoless altruism, while the latter does not?

    Buddha: Yes.

    Vikram: Then, if someone, a wise and perfected one, were to carry out these activities, this worldly journey, with such dispassionate wisdom for the sake of altruism even within a household, then from the perspective of the videha (bodiless) state, there could be no objectionable difference between such a monastic and such a householder, could there?

    Buddha: I concede that the conduct of a videha-mukta (bodiless liberated one) can proceed within a household just as within a monastery. Temptations exist even in monasteries! However, it is evident that they will be greater within a household.

    Vikram: But that fear applies to aspirants, not to the liberated or perfected ones. For we call him an aspirant who still fears temptations, not a perfected one. Now, since the liberated one undertakes this vast enterprise of propagating the dharma and serving the people solely for altruism, for compassion towards the suffering, the main question I wish to have answered by you is whether this worldly altruism is best achieved by indiscriminately giving sanyāsa-dīkṣā (initiation into renunciation) to all, or by becoming a monastic, or by remaining a householder. Is not the principal distinction between the sanyāsāśrama (stage of renunciation) of a videha and the gṛhasthāśrama (stage of householder) of a videha, or, in short, between householdership and renunciation, that in the sanyāsāśrama one must generally make an absolute renunciation of kāminī (woman/sensual desire), kṛṣi (agriculture), and kṛpāṇa (sword), whereas in the gṛhasthāśrama this is not required? By kṛṣi is meant the livelihood of sustenance, by kāminī procreation, and by kṛpāṇa participation in armed conflict when necessary.

    Buddha: Generally, as you say, the renunciant must take a vow of absolute renunciation of kāminī, kṛṣi, and kṛpāṇa; that is indeed the main practical difference between the two āśramas (stages of life).

    Vikram: Then, O Tathāgata, I state explicitly that the work of altruism can be accomplished to a greater extent even by the liberated if they adopt the gṛhasthāśrama rather than the sanyāsāśrama. Not only that, but the recruitment you are making to your renunciate bhikṣusaṅgha, without the slightest consideration of suitability, of all those who wish to become a bhikṣu (mendicant monk), will lead to a great disservice in the transformation of beneficence! Yesterday you admitted Rāhula, your own eight- or nine-year-old son, into the bhikṣusaṅgha! Now consider the enormous calamity that will befall by indiscriminately drawing even tender children, who have no notion of what worldly life or renunciation is, into the renunciate bhikṣusaṅgha! Let me take the case of this very Shākya Nation as an example. You embraced renunciation; you gave sanyāsa-dīkṣā to Prince Ānanda and Devadatta; and finally, Prince Rāhula, that eight-year-old child, too, has been taken into the bhikṣusaṅgha, thus plucking the last bud from the vine of the Shākya royal lineage, which is already nearing its demise. With all the best men, even from the feudal clans, having taken sanyāsa, the growth of excellent progeny from virtuous and noble seed has ceased in this nation. Only the progeny of the vicious and inferior people continued to increase. In such a situation, the same misfortune will befall the Shākya race as befalls the crop of a farmer who merely casts aside good seed and sows only rotten, inferior seeds! If your propagation continues in this manner, with the first vow of sanyāsa-dharma (righteous duty of renunciation), the renunciation of kāminī, and celibacy, leading to such depopulation and decline in good progeny, then hundreds of these people will break their ploughs, abandon their looms, leave their shops, and swell the ranks of beggars in the bhikṣusaṅgha, living off the labour of others! For the abandonment of agriculture is the second vow of sanyāsa-dharma! Let others cultivate the land until they die. To eat that ready-made food is not a sin in sanyāsa-dharma. But farming is a sin, for many creatures die by the plough, hence agriculture is violence-prone! The renunciant himself will not engage in such sinful farming — but he will not refrain from eating the food of those sins, perpetrated by others! It would be less sinful for the renunciant to farm himself and eat that food than this hypocrisy.

    Buddha: Commander Vikram, are not renunciants like me a kind of farmer? Behold, I clear the wilderness of this worldly illusion and plough the field of the mind with the plough of knowledge. Moral principles are the handle of my plough, industriousness is its ox, I sow the seeds of saddharma (true doctrine) tenets, it is watered by the sight of good deeds, and thereafter it yields a crop of eternal life that ends all sorrow!

    Vikram: But, Lord, how many such symbolic farmers like yourself are there in your sanyāsī (renunciant) Saṅgha, who cultivate the nectar-fruit of the supremely auspicious teachings of social virtue? How many are there in this whole world? One, two, at most three. But behold how the economic strength of the nation is weakened by the idleness of the hundreds of unemployed sanyāsī bhikṣu vairāgī (renunciant monks, ascetics, etc.) who gather under the yellow banner of these lone, true farmers! For every person who forsakes worldly life to become a sanyāsī or śramaṇa (ascetic), society loses the earning capacity of two persons – that of the renunciate śramaṇa himself, and that of the worker who supports the śramaṇa. Every śramaṇa is an embodied tax upon every useful profession in society! Take your own Saṅgha! In eight years, thousands of people have joined it. If this continues for a thousand years, that number will reach millions! Furthermore, does not this worldly existence of the renunciant require anything? Though they may not have a double-storied mansion like a householder, they need robes; though not pleasure palaces, they need vihāra (monastic residences); though not temples, they need monasteries; though not modaka (sweets), they need salt and bread. They need sandals, blankets, physicians, medicines, utensils, and even quarrels! Did not the Tathāgata himself often have to threaten to disband the Saṅgha due to the quarrels between opposing factions within it? Lord, just as a household where one brother earns and four merely wander about as idlers is bound to fall into ruin, so too will a society, deprived of the wealth-production of millions and burdened by their upkeep for generations, inevitably fall into ruin. The second vow of sanyāsāśrama, the abandonment of the wealth-producing occupation of agriculture, will have such dire consequences, leading to the misfortune rather than the welfare of the people.

    Buddha: But Commander, material wealth is not the only true wealth. Worldly advancement is not the sole puruṣārtha (aim of human life) of man. You forget that other-worldly advancement is the true puruṣārtha!

    Vikram: Nay! Nay! You have already conceded that the practice of dharma essential for attaining Nirvāṇa, or for the sake of Nirvāṇa, can be achieved even in the gṛhasthāśrama. Therefore, setting aside other-worldly welfare as a common factor for both, it is the comparison of worldly gains and losses that must be made. Indeed, even for the renunciant's existence, the householder's existence is essential. Only if the householder cultivates rice will the renunciant eat by begging for bhikṣā (alms)! If one possesses a detached mind, is there any difference in being bound by spending time begging or spending time farming? Therefore, I say that even liberated ones like yourself could achieve greater public welfare if, instead of binding yourselves with the distorted vows of sanyāsāśrama, you were to live lives of public service within the natural tradition of gārhasthya (householdership). But even if we set aside the question of such extraordinary beings as the Lord, the entry into sanyāsāśrama must be strictly prohibited for all other people. For the two vows a renunciant must take – the absolute renunciation of kāminī and kṛṣi – lead to the decline of population and wealth in a nation, causing harm, as I have just demonstrated. But far more dreadful damage is being inflicted upon this society, this nation, by the renunciant's third vow, the renunciation of the kṛpāṇa, by that deluded ideal of extreme Ahiṁsā (non-violence)! Lord, this is not extreme Ahiṁsā; this is extreme self-destruction! And since the destruction of the self is also violence, extreme Ahiṁsā thus implies extreme violence! Such is the strange import of this foolish definition of Ahiṁsā.

    Buddha: But we are not at all partisans of extreme Ahiṁsā. Not only that, but to resist aggressors with proportionate force and counter-attack for the protection of the righteous and for self-preservation is not violence; on the contrary, it is a meritorious act that thwarts the game of violence. Such is the opinion of the Tathāgata. But this is the duty of the gṛhasthāśrama, not of the sanyāsāśrama!

    Vikram: And that is precisely why I say that the sanyāsāśrama, which requires the absolute renunciation of the sword that protects justice, is the greatest calamity that can befall any nation! A nation where millions of people take oaths not to wield the plough, not to touch weapons, and reside in monasteries, is bound to sooner or later cry out for food, become weak, and fall prey to the sharp claws of some worldly, aggressive nation, like a tiger's pounce! By advocating such renunciation-prone ways, the very worldly existence for whose welfare you, even in your state of liberation, undertake this dharmachakra pravartan and this Saṅgha activity, will surely be utterly destroyed. But in the end, this great religious institution of yours, these Saṅghas, these millions of bhikṣus, these spiritual practices, these meditations, these hermitages—this entire renunciate world—will also be torn asunder and annihilated by the bloody talons of cruel wild beasts and even crueler demons!

    Buddha: But Commander, a renunciant also holds a kṛpāṇa. A weapon it is. Though it be not of iron, it is for that very reason more efficacious than iron. That renunciant's weapon is called Kṣamā (forgiveness)! Vikram, ‘Kṣamā śastraṁ kare yasya śatrūstasya karoti kim?’ (What can an enemy do to him who holds the sword of forgiveness in his hand?)

    Vikram: What? Death! Annihilation of life! Only he who possesses the power to punish can forgive. The forgiveness of the weak, who lack the power to punish, is called surrender—not a weapon! When a cow lies down meekly before a hungry, bloodthirsty tiger, will that tiger spare her, fearing her weapon of forgiveness? Even if that cow says, "I forgive you," the tiger will surely devour her; even if she says, "I surrender," he will devour her! A nation that relies on the foolish hope that continually forgiving wicked enemies will make them cease their aggression will be utterly destroyed by those same enraged enemies, who consider such forgiveness an affront, as soon as an opportunity arises! Such examples have occurred in history, and alas, they are yet to occur! This Shākya Nation of ours is a republic. On one side lies the powerful monarchical state of Kosala, on the other, the monarchical kingdom of Magadha. Between these two powerful monarchical states, the Shākya republic is caught. The moment it becomes even slightly weak, it will inevitably fall prey to the aggression of one of these two vultures. In such a situation, to hold before the people of this nation the distorted ideal that not touching weapons is the excellent way of life, the pinnacle of morality, the sacred path to attaining the Buddha's grace, seems to me utterly self-destructive.

    Buddha: That would be true, were it not that I am striving to turn away even the two aggressive monarchs you cited from warfare; indeed, I have all but done so. See here, does it not seem shameful to you that men should butcher men like vegetables with swords merely to establish superiority in a savage struggle for power? Likewise, if all nations of mankind were to resolve to desist from this shameful thirst for war, if all were to practice the supremely generous principles of my dharma — love, compassion, forgiveness, peace, and Ahiṁsā — then would there be any cause for an individual or a single nation to fear? Behold, it has not been even ten years since the Tathāgata established this Saṅgha, and already King Pradyotana of Kosala and King Bimbisāra of Magadha, whose aggression against our Shākya race you so intensely fear, have both taken initiation into the Tathāgata Buddha's dharma and become our disciples! If the most capable men of the Shākya Nation, like Commander Vikram, were not merely to accept my discipleship but actually embrace bhikṣutva (monkhood) and set forth with me to spread this compassionate dharma, then all these nations would place their trust in our teachings; they would no longer truly feel the necessity of war; they would abhor it; and you would become a partner in the great merit of uprooting war entirely from among humankind! Vikram, is not the goal of eradicating war, strife, and bloodshed from the world, a noble enough endeavour for us to exert our utmost efforts and make the ultimate sacrifices?

    Vikram: Lord, it is indeed noble, noble in the extreme. But I entertain grave doubts as to whether it is possible, and furthermore, whether it is possible by these means.

    Buddha: Commander, regarding that doubt, I say only this: have a little faith! If you will aid me, you shall see the cessation of armed warfare in India within, say, twenty to twenty-five years! You shall witness the era of weapons give way to an era of peace!

    Vikram: May God grant that it be so! The most terrible enemy of mankind, the destroyer of men, is man himself; may this stain upon humanity be washed away as soon as possible, and may the honour of establishing the empire of peace be bestowed upon our Shākyasiṁha Tathāgata Buddha alone! However, gentlemen, with great sorrow yet fearlessly, I must voice my apprehension that not only after twenty-five years, but even after twenty-five hundred years from today, the prevalence of the age of weapons will still be manifest in this world, by its ill fortune; might will continue to be right; the triumphant charger of blood-stained empires will rampage across the earth, and under its hooves, those will be crushed first and foremost who, deeming this dream-utterance of an age of peace a prophecy, rely upon it and are the first to renounce arms, becoming so weak as to depend on the mercy of others! They will swallow the noose of violence for the allure of Ahiṁsā! The dire consequences of this terrible delusion, which admits anyone into the sanyāsāśrama and thereby renders millions of people impotent with the vow of renouncing arms, will be suffered by India for twenty-five generations! Nevertheless, lest future generations mistakenly believe that this experiment failed because a peerless soul like Lord Buddha lacked support in this meritorious endeavour, I shall comply with the Tathāgata's wish; and from this day forth, I join this bhikṣusaṅgha. (Removing the kṛpāṇa from his waist) This sword, dedicated to the service of the Shākyas, and my only son, Vallabha, I entrust to the care of my Shākya race, and I embrace the parivrājaka (wandering ascetic) āśrama.

    Buddha: Commander, this sacrifice of yours for the welfare of the people is commendable, such that even the gods would nod their heads in approval, exclaiming, "Blessed! Blessed!" Behold, come what may, even if history records that we strove to our utmost to establish an empire of peace, compassion, and love in this world, even if humanity gains experience from the outcome of such an experiment, whether successful or not – even that failure must be deemed partially successful! Come, Shariputra, take this honourable newcomer to the Saṅghārāma (monastery) and administer the Saṅgha-dīkṣā (initiation into the Saṅgha).

Verily, man is fond of festivals; even if that festival be of death! Leave aside others, but often, even when someone dies in one's own home, what must the minds of the assembled people be thinking, even amidst the genuine surge of grief? Weeping, such thoughts cannot but arise: "Does she truly grieve, or not? Does he look at me, or not? It would be well if such-and-such person were to see me weeping."     Scene 6

    (Buddha, Senapati Vikramsingh (Bhikshu Vikram), and other bhikshus are seated.)

    Vikram: At last, the fear I harboured forty years ago has proven true! By compassion alone, wickedness; by pity alone, wrath; by non-violence alone, murder; by scripture alone, weapons are not eternally and universally subdued, conquered, or annihilated! Has not this stark truth been brought before our very eyes? Bhagawan, to free mankind from birth, illness, old age, and death, if anyone among men has made the supreme sacrifice, performed severe austerities, and borne immense solicitude, it is you, O Tathagata (one who has thus come), Bhagawan Buddha. The noble path you revealed for the utter annihilation of the threefold miseries (trividha tāpa), and the philosophical conclusion you reached that man attains liberation from the cycle of rebirth through the destruction of craving (tṛṣṇānāśa) – even those materialists who do not believe in these tenets or in your transcendental form of liberation will commend your nature for this much, and remain gratefully indebted, that at least in this life, in this existence, to be bodily free from these four calamities of birth, illness, old age, and death, to be literally completely free, is not within man's power; this much you have certainly proven! However sorrowful it may be, the bound or the enlightened, while possessing a body in this world, must needs remain here, and while in this world, in a physical sense, all mankind, be they bound or liberated in a transcendental sense, must face these four mental and physical afflictions (ādhivyādhi)—this stark truth no human hope shall again dare to call into question. You exerted man's efforts to the very utmost, and before the forces of nature, man's strivings yielded. This, too, was no small gain. Such a defeat is also a partial victory. He who is brave enough to face what is deemed inevitable does not flounder in the tears of his childish desires but braces himself, ready for the fray. Now, if in this physical existence we must face these four foes – birth, illness, old age, and death – then mankind must adopt a strategy of warfare that ensures the least harm and the greatest benefit for one's own side in the struggle against these physical forces. In my opinion, that strategy is Karma Yoga (Karmayoga – path of action), which entails performing all worldly duties properly for the greatest happiness of mankind, or, if you prefer, for the minimization of its sorrow. In your view, Karma Sanyasa (Karmasaṅnyāsa – path of renunciation), the ultimate renunciation of all three – Kāmini (women/desire), Krishi (agriculture), and Kṛpāṇa (weapon) – is the best path to live until death. But has it not now been proven by experience that among these three renunciations, the vow of ultimate weapon-renunciation, at least, is exceedingly detrimental to public welfare? For your lofty teaching of weapon-renunciation will be observed only by the virtuous. Accordingly, they will seek to live solely on the foundations of compassion, non-violence, and forgiveness; while primarily, the wicked and evil will spurn your counsel and seek to dominate by weapon, might, and force. In such contests, the flimsy shield of scripture pages will be crushed by the blows of solid steel, inevitably leading to the destruction of the virtuous and the triumph of the sinful; behold, this prophecy I made forty years ago is coming true today. I do not say this with joy; I say it with profound sorrow. To disprove this inauspicious prediction of mine, against my own inclination, I renounced arms as you bade me and meticulously followed the bhikshu dharma (monastic discipline) according to your command. But ultimately, instead of wars disappearing from a peaceful world, peace itself is on the verge of vanishing from this world amidst the tumult of war. On one side, King Bimbisara of Magadha became your disciple, a devotee of forgiveness and non-violence; but his own son, Ajatashatru, became your adversary; he rebelled against his father and, having slain him, now sits enthroned upon our very bosoms. No sooner had this news from Magadha become old than the news from Kosala arrived, that Vidudabha (Vidhyadgarbha) also pushed his father to the brink of death and seized the royal fortune. That father, King Prasenajit of Kosala, was also your disciple, a follower of your compassion, peace, and non-violence! But your teachings had not the slightest effect on his own son, this Vidudabha, who became your staunch foe! That wicked son rebelled relying on armed force, and that good father, proud of his weapon-renunciation, while fleeing for his life during the rebellion, met his demise. All those virtuous ones who heeded your counsel—King Bimbisara, King Prasenajit, and these Shakyas—were either slain or set upon the path of destruction, while those wicked ones who disregarded your counsel and continued to revere weapons as scripture, prospered. Ultimately, impotent compassion was overcome by wrath; the life of feeble pity was taken by wickedness.

    Buddha: Bhikshu Vikramsingh! Is it your contention that this monastic order (Sangha) of mine has accomplished nothing for the welfare of the people thus far?

    Vikram: Bhagawan, not only this monastic order, but also the sanyasa (renunciate) ashram (stage of life) has conferred immeasurable benefits upon all beings. Indeed, the words spoken by a sanyasi in the forest, having renounced all attachments, have become the scripture of humanity. The saying, 'Dharma (righteous duty) is the foundation of the universe,' is a truth. But neither will this statement be entirely untrue: 'Sanyasa is the foundation of Dharma.' This sanyasa ashram has become as revered as heaven by the touch of such great souls. Taking the example of your own order—indeed, of yourself—for forty years, Bhagawan, through the cool ambrosia of your extremely simple, straightforward, and compassionate religious teachings, the anguish of thousands of beings has been quelled. You proved the futility of sacrifices, thereby saving the lives of hundreds of useful creatures; you dismantled the imaginary tyranny of gods and goddesses, establishing right conduct and public love as the true means of Dharma. Your bhikshus travel from village to village, tending to the sick, assisting the distressed, admonishing against mutual envy, urging mutual compassion, advocating forgiveness of transgressions, and delivering this cool message of contentment to every home. These services of yours, O compassionate Lord, the world can never repay. Yet, even with forty years of the most intense and pure efforts from great souls such as yourself, the truth that 'neglecting armed strength is harmful' has not been disproven. But this apparent failure, at first glance, is actually the successful outcome of those very efforts.

    Ananda: (Entering) Buddham saranam gacchāmi (I take refuge in the Buddha), Dhammam saranam gacchāmi (I take refuge in the Dharma), Sangham saranam gacchāmi. Bhagawan! The direct royal representative of the Shakya republic has come to meet Tathagata Buddha and Bhikshuvar Vikram, bearing the most sorrowful news: King Vidudabha of Kosala has suddenly attacked the unwary Shakya people, utterly devastating them.

    Vikram: What! So it has come to this at last! Ananda! Go, go, bring that Shakya envoy before Buddha Bhagawan, come! (Ananda exits.) Bhagawan, did you not see? Your excessively non-violent teaching may render some individuals compassionate, but it cannot make all forces so. If this is the state of the world even in your resplendent presence, then there is no need to speak of what will transpire after your avatar passes into nirvana. Cruel-natured asuras (demons) will continue to exist in this world, both as individuals and as nations. This is being practically demonstrated. In theory, even if we assume that we could make an entire generation renounce the world, the countless beings who will be born in future generations will have different accumulated karmas (deeds), and thus their innate tendencies will also differ. Therefore, due to a single teaching, some may become compassionate, while others may not. For those millions of beings will have millions of traditions of mental development. Hence, if they are moved by compassion, well and good; but those wicked ones who cannot be so swayed must be restrained from their malevolent mischief by punishment and force. Just as we consider it true compassion to restrain a rabid dog with a stick, we deem it true dharma to punish one wicked creature for the benefit of the greatest number of virtuous creatures. Non-violence is that which severs the fangs and claws of violence with a weapon!

    Shakya Envoy: (Entering) Buddham saranam gacchāmi, Dhammam saranam gacchāmi, Sangham saranam gacchāmi. Bhagawan, the peril that has befallen the Shakya nation is so unbearable, and the message I bring to you is so urgent, that I must convey it all without even awaiting your questions. Senapati (commander of the army) Vikramsingh, the cruel King Vidudabha of Kosala has descended upon the Shakyas like lightning. Our army has been scattered. Only one hero still stands undaunted in battle, holding back Vidudabha—that is, Vikramsingh, your son Vallabhsingh. And in the heart of the dying Shakya nation, there remains but one life-giving hope, one faint flicker of life—that is your aid, O foremost general of the Shakyas, O supreme warrior, O patriarch of the Shakya nation, and the blessings of Bhagawan Buddha! It is our prayer that, though our plea may be inconsistent with the sanyasa ashram adopted by our Vikramsingh, to liberate him in this mortal peril of the Shakya nation, Vikramsingh, you should rush forth like an elephant, considering even the breaking of a vow, like Shri Krishna, as proper in due time for the succour of the distressed. If Vidudabha is not conquered, this Shakya nation will surely perish. And you alone, a warrior, are capable of conquering Vidudabha.

    Buddha: Envoy! Convey our message to the Shakya nation that the Tathagata is deeply grieved by your sorrow. However, equal sorrow is felt for the suffering of Vidudabha and his Kosalan army. For while you are scorched in the fire of their persecution and oppression, they are consumed by the fire of wrath and excessive, envious craving. Both are equally important to us. Our discipline is this: both should abandon enmity!

    Vikram: But ought not the wicked one who initiated the enmity be the one to abandon it? Tomorrow, on behalf of this seat, shall we send an edict to all tigers in the world: 'From the day after tomorrow, cease your enmity towards cattle!' Even when that command is obeyed by tigers, you will find that the wicked and evil among men are not prepared to abandon enmity! Therefore, such wicked ones must be suppressed by force.

    Buddha: But suppression by force is not permanent. Is it not a better way to make the wicked virtuous by dissuading their minds from wickedness?

    Vikram: That may be an excellent path in a vacuum of imagination! Just as the nectar of heaven, obtained after death, is excellent for quelling thirst; but to the thirsty souls of this craving world, a handful of sweet water from a small stream gushing down the village hill offers greater coolness. In poetry, the Kalpavriksha (wish-fulfilling tree) is most excellent; but in actual heat, the humble, ordinary banyan and peepal trees are a hundred times more useful! And secondly, to bring the mind of the wicked into a virtuous state receptive to good counsel, the application of coercive power is often the first effective remedy. Your forty years of experience tell us this: there is a state of wickedness where it does not abandon its evil nature even by the direct commendation of a Buddha. Vidudabha is such a wicked man; therefore, even if his mind cannot be swayed by force, it can certainly be crippled. But mere counsel offers no immediate benefit; it certainly offers no permanent one. If his claws of wrath are clipped, his fangs extracted, then even if he merely lies festering in anger, not truly pacified, this world, at least, will be free from his deadly bloodlust and enjoy fearless peace.

    Buddha: Foremost Bhikshu Vikram, I comprehend the essence of your discourse. But we are sanyasis, those who have renounced worldly pursuits, and the sanyasa dharma advocates non-violence. However, as you assert, since extreme non-violence is simply impossible for humans to practice, and since such ill-timed non-violence, when practiced only by the virtuous before powerful wicked and evil men adopt it themselves, can sometimes be as destructive to the people as violence itself—your proposition is incontrovertible, it cannot be denied. Therefore, we henceforth issue this injunction: my householder followers are permitted to bear arms to fight against aggressive enemies in a just war. Hence, the Shakya nation must necessarily bear arms to resist this incurable Vidudabha's tyrannical aggression. The sin of that violence rests not upon those righteous resistors, but upon those tyrannical aggressors; Manyustann manyumṛcchati ("hatred begets hatred"). O Envoy, go and convey this permission of mine to the Shakyas: may my blessings be a support to your asilatā (sword) in this Dharma-yuddha (righteous war)!

    Envoy: Bhagawan! The entire Shakya nation is profoundly grateful for your blessings. Now, for the Shakya asilatā which you have bestowed these divine blessings to assist, may Bhagawan also present that asilatā to me, so that this envoy may return to the Shakyas at once! Yes, that asilatā of the Shakyas has lain sheathed at your feet for forty years.

    Buddha: The sheathed asilatā of the Shakyas? Where is it? Here?

    Envoy: Yes, Bhagawan! Behold, here! The valour residing secretly like a dagger within the bodily sheath of this Shakya Senapati—this is the national asilatā of our Shakyas! Bhagawan, please place it into our hands now. As soon as the war-conch of this sword-wielding Senapati Vikramsingh is blown on the battlefield, even the jackals in the Shakya forests will become lions.

    Buddha: Meaning, Bhikshuvar Vikram should draw his sword? A sanyasi should drench his robes in blood and make them saffron? Impossible! Envoy, we are sanyasis, detached, impartial; we shall not become involved in your worldly disputes. To us, you are all alike. How could you even conceive that a sanyasi should draw a weapon and go about slaughtering men, and that we should endorse it? Absolute celibacy and absolute renunciation of arms are the two fundamental vows of a sanyasi bhikshu dharma. How can they be broken?

    Vikram: Forgive me! Or do not. But Bhagawan, I must tell you this: if a sanyasi, a detached one, is not to engage in worldly disputes, if we are to treat the just and the unjust as equal, then the greatest transgressor of this injunction of Tathagata Buddha would be Tathagata Buddha himself. Bhagawan, when you were in Rajagriha formerly, a sacrifice was to be performed there where hundreds of lives would be taken. As the herd of sacrificial animals walked along the road, you saw a small lamb limping. Upon seeing it, you were filled with such compassion that you yourself carried the lamb on your shoulders and followed the herd to the royal sacrificial hall, debated with the Brahmins, convinced them that taking lives in sacrifice is a sin, and liberated all the animals, including the lamb. To save one lamb, a hundred animals, from violence, you, the crown jewel of renunciate deeds, engaged in worldly disputes. And here, when hundreds of infant children, hundreds of orphaned maidens, thousands of innocent people are on the verge of losing their lives, you say a sanyasi cannot interfere in worldly disputes! You say that to a sanyasi, both Shakya and Vidudabha are alike. Impartiality is the dharma of a sanyasi! But sanyasi bhikshus peel and eat sugarcane, blow away chaff to eat rice, leave muddy puddles to drink water from good flowing rivers. They put their hands in the udder of a milch cow, but not into a snake's den. Though they eat honey, they do not let nests of poisonous bees cling to their armpits. Thus, we recognize good and evil.

    Buddha: Then, in such a situation, what do you think a sanyasi should do?

    Vikram: What should be done? Before the tiger seizing the cow's neck in its jaws, not a single anuṣṭup (a poetic metre) of the Dhammapada should be chanted, for before that anuṣṭup is complete, the tiger will have already torn the cow's throat and gulped its blood! With one swift stroke, the tiger's head should be severed with a sword! Similarly, a true sanyasi, if necessary, must immediately advance against the army of this wicked Vidudabha with more resolve than any other worldly soldier and kill him. And the lives of thousands of innocent Shakya women and children must be saved. If allowing animal sacrifice to continue for the delusion of attaining heaven is cruelty for a renunciate bhikshu, then would not allowing this human slaughter to continue for the folly of non-violence be a hundred times more cruel, and indeed stain the sanyasi's reputation? Bhagawan, permit me to descend into battle. I still possess the strength to punish the wicked. At the very least, I shall cripple his future cruelty. I shall certainly prevent further grievous violence against the innocent. Nay, perhaps the Shakya nation may yet achieve victory over this tyranny!

    Buddha: What then? Today Vidudabha is conquering the Shakyas. Tomorrow the Shakyas will conquer them. What then? By victory, that enmity will simply continue! He who is defeated will remain sorrowful. Therefore, he who is truly renunciate, truly pacified and tranquil, leaving aside both victory and defeat, sleeps peacefully: “Jayō Vaira Prasuati Dukhe Sete Parājitah. Upasantaḥ Sukhe Sete Hitvā Jayaparājayam.” Translation:

    "Victory breeds enmity; the defeated sleeps in sorrow.
    The pacified sleeps in peace, having abandoned victory and defeat."

    Vikram: Alas, alas! What is this I hear! Bhagawan, when you yourself felt that you had reached Nirvana, did you then continue to lead this body for public welfare, merely out of compassion upon seeing the suffering world, and on the basis of this utterance? Sukhe Sete (sleeps peacefully) – is merely sleeping peacefully the main virtue of a sanyasi? Then you should have long since slept in Nirvana, you should have abandoned your body. If Sukhe Sete implies a figurative meaning for you, then that steadfastness (sthitaprajñatā), that internal renunciation of the fruits of action, is possible in wielding a weapon just as it is in renouncing it. Does not a sanyasi take up a needle to remove a thorn? Then why should he not take up a sword to remove the thorn of evil? If someone ten kos (ancient unit of distance) away suffers a broken thorn, does your heart not ache, and when you go to give them medicine, is your sleep not disturbed, not just figuratively or theoretically, but literally? You say victory breeds enmity, and the defeated unjust one is sorrowful; that is true to some extent. But to avoid the sorrow of defeat for an unjust person, should justice be defeated? Should householders be forced to leave their doors open so that a thief does not experience the sorrow of his theft failing? Or should that sorrow of defeat be considered preferable for the wicked as a punishment for their unjust deeds? It is indeed bad that unjust aggressors experience the sorrow of defeat; but if the rampant injustice of the wicked is allowed to continue, what of the entire human race, tormented and sorrowful by the persecution of innocent virtuous people? That is a thousand times worse! Furthermore, the retribution of punishment that the wicked receive is what first instills fear and curbs their propensity to do evil. And once thus crippled, that evil can be destroyed by good counsel or by habit. The suffering of others is sometimes the indispensable price of the welfare of others. When you left worldly life, how much sorrow Yashodhara experienced; but for the sorrow of your father or wife, you did not return home. When young men or women were initiated into sanyasa and taken into your Sangha, weeping continued in their homes. For this reason, many towns ostracized you and drove you out. Devadatta started a new sect; you opposed him, which made him sorrowful and angry. The enmity grew so much that assassins were sent after you. But you did not accept his sect or disband your Buddha Sangha for the sake of Devadatta's sorrow or to stop that enmity there. Was it not because the sorrow of Devadatta and the growing enmity were indispensable prices for public welfare? If avoiding the suffering of the offender meant keeping millions of innocent people suffering, then why should not the same logic apply to a sanyasi bearing a weapon? When this monster, who did not hesitate to kill his own father for the kingdom, advances with thousands of soldiers against the nation whose food sustains this sanyasi's body daily, at such a time, a sanyasi who, despite believing it possible to slay that monster with a single stroke of the sword, chooses to adjust his blanket rather than wield the sword, so as not to disturb his sleep; who, while millions of women, children, and elders of his nation are being slaughtered, stuffs his ears with rolls of meaningless and destructive vows to avoid having his sleep disturbed by their harsh screams, and 'sleeps peacefully' in deep slumber—a hundred curses upon such a sanyasi! A thousand curses! Sukhe Sete! Is merely sleeping peacefully the goal of a tranquil sanyasi? That may be the goal of pythons, of idlers, of the self-absorbed! Not of sanyasis! Therefore, Bhagawan, command me. Say it, the hourglass of the Shakyas' hope and life has already been turned. So, without delay, say, "Go!" and I shall leap like an eagle, tear apart, break apart, and sever the terrifying coils that the exceedingly fierce serpent of Kosala has wrapped around the Shakyas' lives. Look: but, but let me reach there before the bite. Say, "Go!"

    Buddha: Foremost Bhikshu Vikram, it is astonishing that a philosophic renunciate bhikshu such as yourself should become so agitated all at once. Whatever happens, I cannot bid you, the foremost bhikshu of our Sangha, to take up arms in war and fight. The vow of ultimate weapon-renunciation, upon which the very foundation of the Sangha and sanyasa rests, I shall not allow any bhikshu to break.

    Vikram: Woe to the nation where this feeble sanyasa dharma is considered the supreme Dharma! What else? Shakya envoys, go and tell the royal assembly that Vikramsingh believes that the vow of extreme weapon-renunciation, taken in shortsightedness, was a mistake, and though it is now clear that it causes immense harm to public welfare, to refuse to rectify that error merely to uphold one's own stubborn resolve is not firm determination but sheer obstinacy. However, due to the absence of Bhagawan Buddha's permission, his condition is like that of a snake bound by a charm. He cannot move beyond such limits. Though eager to draw his sword and enter battle for the protection of the Shakyas, I cannot come to your aid...

    Envoy: Ha, ha! Stop for a moment before completing that inauspicious phrase, that cruel verdict, that nation-destroying articulation! You heard the last word of Buddha Deva's will with one ear, but with the other, hear the last word of this afflicted, distressed, dying Shakya nation, and then utter the final word of your decisive sentence! As it is impossible for the tongue of any single individual to express the anguish of the Shakya nation's soul in this dire crisis, its vital struggle, its last hope's final word, this collective tongue of the Shakya people itself shall plead that national sorrow before you! (He removes Vikramsingh's sword and places it before him.)

    Vikram: (Startled) What? My sword! My Kṛpāṇa! Forty years have passed since I took sanyasa, when I laid this down! With this sword, I have vanquished enemies twelve times!

    Envoy: Senapati, as a monument to those twelve victories, we Shakyas always adorn its hilt with twelve jewels and keep this sword hanging in our royal assembly as our national treasure. Every new Senapati bows to it and accepts his position. This is that sword! The Shakyas have dispatched this sword as our representative to convey our message to you, to plead our request, and to sway your heart. If the Shakya nation is now destined to die, let it at least die resisting bravely; but even that is possible only if you take up this sword and descend into battle instantly! Maharaj, for a moment, bring before your eyes the scene I have witnessed. Behold, entire villages—Amrapalli, Kedanavati, Limbaniya—are ablaze, set alight by the Kosalan armies! See the old men and children being crushed and burned alive beneath collapsing, flaming beams and roofs! The acrid stench of their burning flesh spreads for miles! See hundreds of Kosalan soldiers, shouting "Kill! Strike!", running with naked swords, slashing, looting, and trampling every Shakya they encounter! See, they have ridden their horses over those clusters of women, those children trampled! Those splatters of blood, those severed heads, those screams, that fleeing, that groaning! Maharaj, the Shakya nation is your child. This butcher Vidudabha and his wicked general Chanda are approaching its back with a dagger! That crying, frightened child clings to your waist. Have pity, save it!

    Vikram: I shall save it! Let the Shakya sanyasi who can sleep peacefully while his nation faces such devastation do so! I, for one, cannot! Bhagawan, you heard it all, did you not? Then may I not take up this sword?

    Buddha: This is entirely contrary to sanyasa dharma, to bhikshu dharma! I wish to say no more than this.

    Vikram: Then, without your saying it, behold! I renounce this feeble, nation-destroying, human-welfare-opposing sanyasa dharma itself! Behold! I take up this sword of duty! My Shakya nation! O suffering humanity! For the sake of national dharma, for the sake of human dharma, for the sake of compassion for all beings, as an indispensable means, this sanyasi takes up this weapon. Behold! I take up this weapon in such circumstances that thereby, even if Vidudabha's cruelty is not vanquished, it can certainly be crippled, and I can save thousands of lives. Through the renunciation of arms in the past forty years, I could not achieve as much welfare for the people nor free as many beings from suffering as I shall be able to achieve through bearing this weapon in the coming forty days! I go. Every moment of delay further entangles the Shakyas' neck in the noose of death. Yet, O all creation, you are my witness that I am leaving this sanyasa ashram, crippled by vows that are occasionally detrimental to public welfare; and since that extreme non-violence which leads to the protection of the wicked and the destruction of the virtuous is not dharma but actual adharma (unrighteousness), I abandon it; not Bhagawan Buddha! (He exits.)

    Vidyudgarbha: Quite right. Concurrently, we must also pursue our policy of ensuring that Buddha becomes most revered and beloved among the Shakyas. For, as the Shakya nation suffered impoverishment, population decline, and loss of strength by increasingly adhering to the principles of sannyasa-pravana (inclined towards renunciation) Buddhism, so shall it continue to decline and become increasingly feeble. Therefore, addressing the Shakyas, I shall issue a proclamation of the following purport as an indirect answer to this question: that Kosaleshvara holds Sri Gautama Buddha in high esteem. No worldly attachment could sway him from his sannyasa-dharma (path of renunciation). His vow of shastrasannyasa (renunciation of arms), ahimsa (non-violence), and compassion, he kept unbroken even in such trying circumstances. Truly desireless and liberated is one such as he. Even the enemies of the Shakyas hold him in great reverence. But for this very reason, we are deeply saddened that the Shakyas, who gave birth to such a noble soul, have forsaken adherence to him and instead follow that sinful, fallen Vikramsimha, who disobeyed Lord Buddha's command, insulted him, broke his sacred vows of renunciation, and brought disgrace upon his own clan's name. Hence, if the Shakyas, by expelling Vikram, who insulted Lord Buddha, and by jatibahishkrit (excommunicating him from their caste) him as fallen, surrender their capital into our hands within eight days, then we shall not destroy them. Thereupon, not a single drop of blood shall be shed. Otherwise, to punish Vikram, his son, Commander Vallabhsimha, who has fallen into the hands of our victorious army, shall be immediately put to death. We require an answer forthwith.

    Kosala Commander: Bravo! Most appropriate. This proclamation should be published here, and this army, held in check by Vikramsimha, should be kept stationed thus. But another army should be dispatched to assault their capital, Kapilavastu, by a circuitous route to launch a secret attack. If the Shakyas surrender, then they can easily be annihilated and utterly wiped out.

    Vidyudgarbha: Should the Shakyas fall into this snare of surrender, my stratagem shall succeed with ease. Even if they fight, it shall still succeed. Come what may, my thirst for vengeance, unquenched by the Shakyas' persecutions until now, I shall surely satisfy by seizing the throat of the Shakya capital!

Just then, four rogues like yourselves came to the village, proclaiming that upon entering the dharma (righteous duty) of Lord Buddha, one becomes free from all sorrows! No old age, no disease, no death! So I was swayed. I thought I would remain thus, straight-backed, charming-eyed, with a graceful gait, the very image of youth, for life! But having become a bhikshuṇī, I find it all empty? And if one wishes for a good bhikshu for companionship, this is your sort of pair! A distressed Nārayaṇa—!     Shākambhaṭa: As for doubled stomach pain, that would be the inevitable result of such dual companionship. If that be a flaw, it belongs to the law of nature, not the law of Buddha! And there was an excellent remedy to avoid old age.

    Bhikshuṇī: Tell me, tell me, Shākambhikshu, tell me still. What is that excellent remedy to never grow old in this life?

    Shākambhaṭa: To give up one's life in youth! It was thy folly to rush with the axe of Buddha's dharma instead of using this needle to remove the thorn of old age rooted in thy foot! What can bhikshus do about that? The same holds for thy other hopes concerning this dharma! Dost thou not understand that every dharma in the world, in presenting hopes of removing all human suffering, of freeing people from birth, old age, disease, and death—all such promissory notes it issues are in pāralaukika (otherworldly) terms, not in the language of this world. How can the birth these dharmas seek to avoid be this very birth? For this birth hath already come and passed! Naturally, all these are hopes for the next birth. All such promissory notes of all dharmas are always written for encashment at the bank of the otherworld. And since people are dazzled when they try to read the divine address on them with open eyes, it may perhaps only be readable after death hath closed their eyes! All these wish-fulfilling cows of dharma are tied in Yama's cowshed, their wish-fulfilling trees are planted in the gardens of cremation grounds, and alas, all their heavens are visible only after death! If, tomorrow, a dharma were truly to emerge that literally alleviated all suffering in this life, in this world, such that its follower would not fall ill even with improper diet, would not suffer decrepitude even in old age, and death would not answer even if called—then, foolish girl, would there be any need to send thousands of bhikshu missionaries to thy very doorstep for the propagation of such a dharma? To join such a dharma, people from this world, nay, even from the moon-world, would walk to that dharma-temple, and even if ye were to try to close the doors of that temple due to the terrible crush, people would rise in rebellion, shouting, 'Such a dharma is our birthright!' But among all these dharmas, there is no moneylender who would pay the sum of these promissory notes in the currency of this life, cash down. Like answers whose verification cannot be checked, all these dharmas are equally true or false.

    Shākambhaṭa: And what then! Alas, Buddha well knows how contrary-hearted women are. Did not that Chichabayā create a great commotion, alleging that Buddha himself had such intimate relations with her? And what I say against dharma, that any religion's promise to literally and in this very life free humans from birth, disease, old age, and death is merely a lie—that I can prove from Buddha's own example. First, birth—Buddha himself experienced it. As for diseases, there's no need to speak. Initially, he used scattered grave-cloths from cremation grounds. But when terrible diseases began to arise from them, he commanded bhikshus to use new clothes and himself began to wear costly robes gifted by kings. At first, taking medicines was considered a sin; but later, even royal physicians began to visit. And to save his own body, the proponents of ahiṃsā (non-violence) did not hesitate to allow surgeons to kill millions of germs. What a royal figure he appeared in his youth! If he had completed his incarnation then, at least foolish people would have said he did not fall into the hands of old age. But as if to prevent even that boast, destiny granted him a long life and now hath delivered him into the hands of old age as well. Like any other old man, his teeth began to fall out, his limbs became hollow. Old age took its revenge, debilitating him who had proudly set out to eradicate old age from the entire world! And as for death, Lord Buddha himself now, as he comes and goes, tells the bhikshu community that his death is near, and is making arrangements for the saṅgha after his passing!     Shākambhaṭa: Nay, "useless" means cast aside by all others, not that which I find to be cast aside. Why should our hand reach for those whom everyone desires! That is why bhikshus consider even a skull, cast aside by all, a worthy receptacle! (A bell rings.)

    Shākambhaṭa: Meaning, it is time for my dhyāna (meditation). Go inside.

    Bhikshuṇī: Oh, now he's making a fuss about me! Goodness gracious. I go, I'll be right back! Goodness gracious! (She goes.)

Be it merely sweeping, but even that is work, and by evading it, I strive to the best of my ability to become completely non-attached, which is in accordance with the bhikshu-dharma. Here comes someone to call me! If I do not quickly close my eyes and assume the posture of dhyāna, I shall have to rub my eyes and assume the posture of a broom! Yes, is not the art of deeply sleeping while sitting also an accomplishment! Oh my, he's here. (He closes his eyes, just as a Bhikshu enters. As the Bhikshu proceeds, Shākambhaṭa squints his eyes, and when the Bhikshu suspiciously looks back, he closes them again. After the Bhikshu leaves.) That nuisance is gone! But he seemed suspicious today. Now, merely squinting with dhyāna will not suffice. The first external test of dhyāna is having closed eyes. But the final test is not coming to consciousness even if some wretched bhikshuṇī tickles one. Such dhyāna must be achieved today! And its direct guru-killi (master key) (He takes out a bhāṅga pill.) —this old acquaintance of mine, which, unlike the quackery of gurus, dissolves all sorrows right here, right now, in this very body, and therefore is more worthy than even gāṃjā to be renowned later in common parlance as bhāṅga (hemp paste)—this pill! —Come, divine bhāṅga, fall into my throat with a plop, like a fishing lure into a well! (He swallows the pill.) Oṃ! Oṃ! Ah, how the outer eyes of the mind are closing, and the inner eyes of the mind are opening. What strange, varied waves! How I float along. Could this not be savikalpa samādhi (meditation with support), which reveals extraordinary visions? The methods differ, but the state is one. Am I truly making progress in Yoga-Shastra? And oh, I am losing myself! Oh, hold, hold! I have slipped down the descending, slippery slope of nirvikalpa samādhi (meditation without support). (He sprawls on the ground.) Sleep, wilt thou at least let me wake after the monastery work is done? Who says scriptural verses are false! Behold, the exact experience! All work avoided: naiṣkarmyasiddhiṃ paramāṃ acireṇādhigacchati (he quickly attains the supreme perfection of non-action). (He sprawls completely.)

    Bhikshuṇī: (Suddenly enters.) Goodness gracious! It seems the gentleman has fallen asleep while pretending to meditate. Let me apply the unguent of tickles and expose all this pretense, just wait. (She tickles him.) Oh my, truly deep meditation! It is not broken even by tickling! Could it be abhaṅga samādhi (unbroken meditation)? Then I must inform the sthaviras (elder monks). They themselves stand guard over those who attain abhaṅga samādhi to protect their bodies. Oh, Revered Sthavira! Come, come! Shākambhikshu hath attained abhaṅga samādhi! Abhaṅga samādhi!

    Shākambhaṭa: (Jumping up abruptly.) Who says this is abhaṅga samādhi? This is not abhaṅga samādhi. This is sabhaṅga samādhi (broken meditation). When it is attained by the grace of divine bhāṅga, I shall not be ungrateful to bhāṅga by calling it abhaṅga samādhi; say sabhaṅga samādhi! Sabhāṅga samādhi! Say sabhāṅga!

    Bhikshuṇī: (Clamping her and his mouth.) Hush! Hush! Softly! What if the sthavira hears, Shākambhikshu! Regain your senses! Regain your senses!!

    Tākasiṃha: (Enters.) Stop, I have come to bring that thief to his senses. Vile Bhaṭuraḍyā, speak. Whose khaḍāva (wooden sandals) are these? Thief, thou hast stolen my khaḍāva! Last time, thou didst steal my begging bowl and got away with it. But now, I shall take thy life. Bring those khaḍāva!

    Shākambhaṭa: (Struggling.) Those are my khaḍāva! And what if they were thine? Thou art a bhikshu, yet thou wouldst give thy life for khaḍāva?

    Tākasiṃha: I shall! Even if my life is lost for the khaḍāva on my feet, so be it! (He takes one khaḍāva after another and keeps striking him fiercely.)

    Bhikshuṇī: Goodness gracious! Just as Sunda and Upasunda fought over Mohini in ancient times, so these two will surely lose their lives fighting over these khaḍāva! Oh, Shākambhikshu (He pushes her away.) Goodness gracious! Oh, Tākambhikshu (He also pushes her away.) Goodness gracious!

    Girl: (From behind the curtain.) Save me, save me. (She enters.) Bhikshus, save me. I am a princess from the Shākya royal family. The demons from the Kōsala army are running after me, either to make me a slave to General Chaṇḍa or to kill me. I must find abhaya (protection) in this Buddha vihāra (monastery)!

    Shākambhaṭa: Fear not, girl! (Still beating with the khaḍāva.) If any soldiers enter this vihāra, I shall chop them up thus! I shall finish them off!

    Soldier: (Enters.) What says thou, beggar?

    Shākambhaṭa: (Trembling.) Nothing! According to the rules of Buddha vihāras, armed soldiers do not enter. Since she appeared helpless, I said that if they entered, I would finish them off. If I had known you would enter, I would not have spoken so at all.

    Soldier: Thou art lying! Hey coward! Hey coward!

    Shākambhaṭa: (To Bhikshuṇī.) Hey woman! Answer him, he is calling you!

    Bhikshuṇī: Goodness gracious! I am not among them.

    Soldier: Coward, not her, but thee—thou art lying.

    Shākambhaṭa: Shame, shame! We bhikshus, ahiṃsā (non-violence) is our principal vow. We consider even uttering violent words like "strike," "beat," "thrash" a sin. So, brave warriors, how can we tell lies? No, Master, we observe the vow of ahiṃsā so strictly that we do not even "beat" lies, do not even "thrash" idle talk, do not even "cut" gossip, do not even "kill" a call. Forgive us! You should take this girl and depart safely. We shall go inside. Our time for chanting the rosary of ahiṃsā is drawing near.

    Girl: But bhikshus, is not dayā (compassion) also your vow, like ahiṃsā? My suffering...

    Shākambhaṭa: We feel dayā. But, girl, if we rescue thee, then the wrath of these soldiers and the lust of General Chaṇḍa will remain unfulfilled, causing them suffering. Should we not feel dayā for them too? As we feel such dayā for the suffering of both of you, we do not meddle in the affairs of relieving anyone's suffering. We shall go inside. Our time for chanting the rosary of dayā is drawing near!

    Soldier: Who is that hiding behind the bhikshuṇī? Pull him out!

    Tākasiṃha: It is I! I am not a cowardly Brahmin like this Shākambhaṭa, but a Kshatriya. Your own caste-brother! (He clasps his hands in supplication.)

    Soldier: Oh, oh! Hadst thou been some lifeless and worthless Brahmin, we might have spared thee as a mere jester; but thou art a Shākya Kshatriya! At a time when this Shākya nation of thine is being trampled under the hooves of our Kōsala horses, even if thou hadst fought against us in battle, enraged by the defeat of thy nation, we might have spared thee. But...

    Shākambhaṭa: But if you look at it that way, when he was formerly a Kshatriya, there was no war. Since the war began, he has been a bhikshu. And we bhikshus have no caste, no nation. Let any win in this war between Shākyas and Kōsalas! What is that to us? Whosoever wins, him shall we call king. Beyond this, we have no concern whatsoever with that victory or defeat. It is Buddha's own command that worldly life is supreme suffering; what wise man would commit the sin of war for that, and even if he did—

    “जयो वैरं प्रसवति दुःखे शेते पराजितः । उपसन्तः सुखे शेते हित्वा जयपराजयम्“।। Therefore, we sukhe shete! We sleep in happiness.

    Soldier: Alas, alas! O wretches! Though I may not agree with all the views of Lord Buddha, seeing these sprinkles of the sacred Vāgbhāgīrathī, flowing from the sthitaprajñatā (steadiness of wisdom) of that Tathāgata Mahatma Buddha, like a Himalaya, thus defiled upon falling into the puddle of these wretches' minds, I cannot but hang my head in shame. But a moment ago, I saw you two bhikshus fighting over mere khaḍāva by stealing them, and now to hear you say that fighting is a sin when the ruin of your own nation and your own caste is at hand, and to see you hide your cowardice beneath the shield of Buddha's ahiṃsā—it makes me wish to behead you instantly. We shall eradicate such adharma (unrighteousness) of this ahiṃsā-dharma (religion of non-violence) from the kingdom root and branch.

    Shākambhaṭa and Tākasiṃha: But we are bhikshus, inviolable! You know the scriptures! Nainaṃ chhindanti shastrāṇi (Weapons cannot cleave it).

    Soldier: Hypocrites! Do ye take me for some simpleton ass, with two long ears of wicked pity and meaningless ahiṃsā, who would let such deceitful bhikshus live as bhikshus! Seize them, put nose-rings on them, and harness them to forced labour. And then, place them at the forefront of battle, to act as a shield, until their bodies are riddled like sieves, to waste as many enemy arrows as possible.

    Tākasiṃha: (Clinging to Shākambhaṭa's neck.) Alas, my friend, even in the monastery, death does not leave Kshatriyas alone!

    Shākambhaṭa: Still, O Kshatriya, thou shouldst not weep first; it is proper that I, a Brahmin, should weep first, and then thou, a Kshatriya, shouldst wail. Alas, alas! Oh, do not push me so. Now, indeed, there is no doubt that calamity has befallen the Shākya nation, my friend! Alas, alas! (As soldiers push them away, he suddenly turns back.) Oh, but you have left that bhikshuṇī! At least take her along?

    Bhikshuṇī: Goodness gracious, am I thy wedded wife to commit sati with thee, thou wretch? Goodness gracious! (She goes.)

    Scene 4

    The War Council – Vikramsingh, Sulochana (Sulochana in disguise), and two other Military Commanders.

    Vikram: Commanders of the remaining last army of the Shakyas, the main reason for this urgent war council, which I have convened amidst the thick of battle, is the extremely important letter we have received from our enemy, Vidyutgarbha of Kosala, as you are aware. An answer to that letter must be given at this very moment. This moment of answering that letter will likely be the last moment of our Shakya nation’s life! For if you decide that it is more expedient to lay down arms and surrender unconditionally, as demanded by Vidyutgarbha's letter, then the Shakya kingdom is dead at this instant. But even if you do not decide thus and continue the war, the Shakyas can endure for, at most, another four or six days. This is because the deadly consequences of the long-standing national neglect of military strength, a sin that we committed, must now be suffered by us, no matter how much you or I struggle at this eleventh hour—there is no alternative! Although we have held Vidyutgarbha’s main army here, he possesses many subsidiary forces. We have just now received secret intelligence that one of these is advancing upon our capital. Therefore, even if we continue the war, victory is not possible. The only satisfaction we shall now gain is to make the enemy half-dead before we fully die ourselves, not letting him achieve victory too easily. And that too is a heroic duty; so speak. The decision you utter at this moment will be the final word of our nation; bear this eternal responsibility in mind and speak. Vidyutgarbha’s first proposal is that he holds great reverence for Bhagawan Buddha. For Buddha not only preached the principles of disarmament, non-violence, and compassion, but also, while such a conflagration of war raged all around, he remained unentangled in the world’s snares, steadfastly practicing these principles as before. This monastic devotion to disarmament is extremely venerable. Buddha is a great personality in the world.

    First Commander: If Vidyutgarbha truly finds this disarmament commendable, then first, let him himself cease his present violence and show compassion by adopting such disarmament today!

    Sulochana: In one sense, disarmament is indeed venerable. The Shakyas are now even considering it imitable. For the disarming of a weapon—meaning its throw—that accurately severs the neck of a villain, that is disarmament. That throw of an arrow, that strike of a sword, that thrust of a khadga (sword)—these too are venerable to us. We shall imitate them.

    Vikram: Vidyutgarbha’s second proposal in this final ultimatum is that I, who have deviated from Buddha’s venerable vow, and, though a monk, have broken the vow of disarmament and taken up arms in violent warfare, should be severely punished by you for this transgression of Buddha’s command and breach of vow.

    Sulochana: Indeed, a punishment has been meted out. The harshest of all punishments, as soon as you were ready to bear arms, O armed ascetic, the Shakyas have indeed given to you. For the Shakyas have bestowed upon you the royal scepter, which is the very embodiment of all punishments, and appointed you the supreme and unique ruler of the nation.

    First Commander: O young warrior, you have spoken absolutely correctly. And in bestowing that royal scepter upon an armed ascetic, it is true that not only the commander, as Vidyutgarbha says, but we too have somewhat deviated from our vows. For with the exception of one Vallabha, we Shakyas, who were bewildered by the sudden raid of the Kosalas, and who, without fighting a single battle, held military strength in low esteem and thus became even weaker, had taken a vow of constant retreat before the enemy and of merely being killed. But as soon as this ascetic commander, Vikramsingh, entered the fray, we ceased to observe that vow. O supreme hero, it is because of your coming that we have been able to utterly destroy the Kosala army for these four days and somewhat crush the cruel Vidyutgarbha’s fangs—that indeed has been our deviation from the vow!

    Second Commander: When the very enemies of Bhagawan Buddha, who had four times sent assassins against him, come forward to declare that Buddha is the greatest man in the world, it merely proves that non-resistance and extreme non-violence benefit these aggressors. He holds up Buddha's ideal to us, not to himself! If all of us become such non-resistant Buddhist monks, then this Shakya land will fall easily into his lap. And if we become followers of resistant armed non-violence, as you advocate, then such violence as his father did not face, this Vidyutgarbha also will not face. Therefore, he seeks to create disrespect for us in our minds, and puts on a hypocritical display about Buddha. Tell Vidyutgarbha, if Buddha is the greatest man in the world, then let him himself become a follower, embrace non-violence, and become a monk!

    Vikram: Now, after these two introductory proposals, I seek your opinion on the main proposal in Vidyutgarbha’s final ultimatum. It would be well if the Shakyas were to send an unconditional letter of surrender right now and cede the kingdom to Vidyutgarbha. Otherwise, he says, he will immediately behead Commander Vallabha, who has fallen into their hands, and inflict an extremely severe punishment upon you, such as the punishment I have meted out to the Shakyas thus far is nothing compared to, as soon as your capital falls into my hands! I will slaughter the Shakyas, including their women and children. Tell me what answer we should send to this dreadful threat. It is human nature for my heart to be swayed by paternal affection on such an occasion, Vallabha being my son. My opinion will influence you, so I shall not offer any opinion in this decision but will decide according to your majority. Only if by chance you fail to reach a definitive collective opinion will I consider my authoritative opinion as the supreme ruler to be the decisive one. Gentlemen, this is a time of total destruction for a nation. Therefore, without letting the balance tilt towards either thoughtless bravery or thoughtless cowardice, speak clearly whatever you have to say.

    First Commander: Commander, after three days of close combat, it is needless for me to say that I do not fear my own death. I have personally counted and sent my four sons into this battle; I saw them killed. But now the question before me is whether I should witness this entire Shakya race being slaughtered or not! Commander, even if we fight further, the capital will fall; the enemy is extremely powerful; who can achieve something beyond human effort? In the last three or four days, we have exerted the utmost bravery. But now, when there is not the slightest hope of resistance remaining, merely to die for death’s sake and cause the destruction of thousands of orphaned and helpless Shakya people is almost a sin, so it seems to me. Let me be called a coward by this generation or a future one, I care not! That nationalism which causes the slaughter of thousands of people in a nation merely to be called brave is not nationalism, but treason! As long as war could serve the interests of the Shakyas, I waged war. Now, since surrendering to the enemy will avert, to some extent at least, the misfortune of the Shakyas, I consider it my true duty to surrender unconditionally to Vidyutgarbha.

    Second Commander: If even these noble Shakya commanders see no other way but for the Shakyas to surrender, what more can I say?

    Vikram: Both your opinions will form the majority. That is the decision, so is surrender appropriate?

    Second Commander: Nay, nay, I do not say it is appropriate! I only say it is unavoidable. How can I say surrender is appropriate! Alas, for the Shakyas to surrender means to strip our kingdom, our republic, our regal raiment from the Ikshvaku branch of the solar dynasty. This royal crown of fifty-two generations is to be kicked off our heads by Vidyutgarbha’s foot. It is to be placed in some exhibition in Kosala’s capital, like a toy for women and children to behold during festivals! That is what surrender means. We are to be bound by chains or led by guards before Vidyutgarbha, who will twist his mustache and say, “What, you rascals! Are you tired of fighting?” And with a furious kick, we are to stumble and fall far away! In every marketplace and square of the world, people will say, “The Shakya kingdom is ruined! What fools! They went to establish non-violence in the world and ended up destroying their own spirit! They lost their kingdom without fighting a single battle! What cowards!” And those words I shall hear wherever I go! Bah, surrender is not appropriate, it is not appropriate, it is not appropriate, and even if unavoidable, it matters not. But then, should a gem like Vallabha be allowed to die for the sake of this hollow pride of not surrendering? Hollow pomp, for without reason, that Shakya canopy, that palace, that crown, will be crushed under the victorious hooves of Vidyutgarbha’s horse. The world will say the Shakya nation is dead, so why sacrifice Vallabha? By what right? Vallabha, our valiant commander, who rallied the bewildered, disorganized Shakya army as best he could, stemmed the enemy’s tide, and when all remedies failed, shouting "Strike! Kill!", threw himself into the enemy and fought until surrounded and captured—are the Shakyas to send a thankless message to Vidyutgarbha, saying, “You may happily behead him”? With such treachery, how can I show my face to Vallabha’s loving and young wife, whose neck we would metaphorically sever? May your house repay the debt of three generations of martyrs by rendering your lineage childless! And may we be instrumental in the enemy, enraged, turning the remaining Shakya race into ashes! Merely for hollow pride! Bah, Commander! We must surrender! No, we must not surrender! Both paths are equally contemptible; equally acceptable. What then shall we decide? Grandfather, I leave everything to you. I have no opinion. Your opinion is my opinion.

    Vikram: O young warrior, now speak your mind. Three days ago you joined the army as an unknown soldier. In these three days, by your utmost display of valor, the lightning speed of your horse, the accurate aim of your arrows, and the ruthless courage of your heart, you have risen from soldier to commander in three days. I consider you a representative of the young generation of Shakyas. Speak. Now the entire decision rests upon your opinion. For of the three of you, one has offered no opinion. One has given the opinion that surrender is appropriate. Now, if you too give the same opinion, then by your majority, surrender will be the decided course. Commander Vallabha's life now depends not on Vidyutgarbha’s sword, but on your tongue. Vallabha’s young beloved stands at the door, waiting anxiously for him, remember her. Picture hundreds of crimson Kosala swords, drunk with the intoxication of blood, invading the streets of the Shakya capital, cutting down Shakya men, women, and children at sight! Then speak. The inclination of your youthful nature will quickly rise, roaring "War! War!!" befitting an enraged warrior. That side will be your natural tendency, therefore, to rein in that fiery passion, I am putting forth the counter-argument more extensively. Now, state your definite decision.

    Sulochana: National Patriarch, Supreme Authority, I state my definite opinion in clear words. Hear me! If we lay down arms today and surrender unconditionally, I find no specific boon Vidyutgarbha offers us in his letter. He will take the capital. He will destroy the Shakya kingdom, he will make the Shakya crown a footrest for the throne of Kosala. Not only that, but he gives no promise in that letter that he will not continue the same destruction—burning Shakya villages, indiscriminate slaughter, laying waste the country—that he has wrought until now, after taking the capital. My two senior commanders must not have realised this. Therefore, they assume that if we surrender, such a catastrophe will not befall the nation further. However, Vidyutgarbha does not say he will not cause a worse catastrophe than what has occurred so far. All he says in that letter is that if you do not surrender, a more terrible catastrophe than any until now will befall you! But what could be a more terrible catastrophe than the torment we have already endured? Furthermore, consider that the word of Vidyutgarbha, who here engaged in talks of peace and then suddenly launched a more insidious attack on the thus unsuspecting Shakyas, is nothing but deception. Therefore, the situation in brief is this: the Shakya nation’s destruction is mostly certain. Hope of victory is lost; yet, the Shakyas still have one hope. That is, of revenge. Avoiding death is impossible, but one thing is still possible. That is, to die fighting!

    First Commander: This attitude of yours is merely a blind reaction of anger.

    Sulochana: It is indeed so. But this reaction of suppressed anger is the goad to the elephant of madness. If one steps on a snake's tail, the snake quickly turns and bites, and as we die, we render the enemy mortally wounded, not for victory, but for revenge. And because mankind fears that the snake's species is vengeful, people do not dare to crush a snake as easily as children crush an ant or a lizard. An arrow-wounded tiger, even when dying, leaps with this desperate reaction and sinks its teeth into the hunter’s throat. The tiger dies; yet, seeing this vengeful reactive nature of the tiger, no man goes tiger hunting as casually for fun as he would go rabbit hunting. What is an ant! When it bites a man’s foot, it knows full well that by this bite it will not personally survive. But it bites not to live, but to ensure that the one who killed it must suffer some punishment. That biting ant dies. But then, not only man but even snakes rarely enter a nest of such biting ants of its kind! There is a species of bees. As soon as their hive is destroyed, they abandon the pursuit of honey, caring not what becomes of their other bees. Instead, each individual bee will relentlessly pursue the person who destroyed the hive, stinging them. So firmly does its stinger embed itself in the person's body that it cannot retreat. It dies there. Yet, it knowingly goes to die thus. For the satisfaction of dying in revenge gives it greater joy than the satisfaction of a humiliated life. Consequently, mankind never fears an entire colony of silkworms as much as it fears a single one of those bees. This sting of revenge that nature has placed in the anger of all creatures has such a divine purpose: that this tendency for revenge does not let an aggressive assault and its sinful plunder go completely uncostly. Thus, the aggressor does not rush to commit such acts with the same fearless speed as he would if there were no fear of revenge. The rest of the world remains safer from aggressive torment to that extent. Therefore, if the Shakya nation’s death is now inevitable, then not for its own life, but at least for the rest of the world to live, the Shakyas should fall upon Vidyutgarbha like tigers. Each Shakya should die, exacting revenge and meting out punishment, crying out, at least five cruel Kosalas, so that this Vidyutgarbha will not dare to launch such an armed raid on other small, similar principalities in the future. He will not be able to achieve victory so easily! At such a time, revenge is not merely an innate reaction, but a benevolent duty. Therefore, my answer to your question is unequivocally one: Not surrender, but revenge!

    Vikram: O young warrior, I offer this caution not to obstruct your decision, but that there may be no diminishment in the heroic and spontaneous reasoning with which you have supported that decision. You have, considering all other causes and effects, omitted one crucial thing entirely. We do not lay down arms. Our answer is not surrender. Revenge! The moment the sound of your decision reaches the ears of that wicked Vidyutgarbha, his sword, too, will fall upon Vallabha’s neck with the terrible impact of its echo, and his head will be severed instantly! You have not forgotten this, have you? It is a different matter if you attach no importance whatsoever to that single consequence.

    Sulochana: Who counts an individual in the consideration of the eternal gains and losses of nations! Patriarch, I cannot make the mistake of considering that thought.

    First Commander: What understanding or misunderstanding can such an unknown youth have! How can he comprehend the importance of Commander Vallabha! You are a warrior, but you are an ignorant youth, unacquainted with statecraft, dwelling in some village. O brave youth, have you ever even seen that steadfast Commander Vallabha? Ah, if you had seen his charming, divine countenance, alas, at the very word of your decision, your mind's eye would have seen that radiant countenance severed by the wicked Vidyutgarbha’s axe and falling to the ground, and you would have fainted. Who counts an individual? But a nation is not some independent entity floating in the sky! The nation of an individual, and especially an individual like Commander Vallabha, marked by extraordinary personal greatness and vested with the authority of national commandership, is a manifest expression of the nation itself! One of these two lineages, whose history is the history of the Shakyas over the last three centuries, is our royal lineage; it was severed and rendered extinct by the ascetic tongue of Bhagawan Buddha. This second national lineage, next to the royal lineage, is the ancestral line of these Patriarchs. That lineage is being severed and rendered extinct today by your warlike tongue. Alas, alas, and behold Vallabha's young new bride, Sulochana! Maharaj, she is my niece. I saw her when she was a child. She shone like a star! Later, we went far away, and she never met me again. She has not yet even passed her twentieth year. Not even a full year may have passed since her love-marriage! Before the first bloom of flowers has even appeared on her vine of youth, how dare you try to wipe away the tender vermilion of her hopes of happiness! Do not sever the throat of that delicate-hearted maiden with a hair! Young man, do you have any beloved person in this world for whom your soul yearns when you do not meet them? If so, bring that beloved image before your eyes. Then immediately envision the gruesome scene where that beloved person’s dear countenance is sharply severed from the neck by a butcher’s axe, and thick gouts of blood spurt forth; and then remember that the anguish that will overcome your heart with the imagined pain of that scene will be a hundredfold less than the agony Vallabha's beloved new bride, Sulochana, will experience upon hearing the news of his actual beheading, and then speak! What more can I say than this?

    Sulochana: I said earlier that in matters of national or global welfare, the question of individual joy and sorrow is secondary. But if you insist on my opinion even on that secondary question, then I ask, even if we lay down arms and the enemy perhaps spares Vallabha’s life, for what purpose would he spare him? When that Vidyutgarbha returns to Kosala after annihilating this Shakya nation, will it not be to parade Vallabha as the conquered commander of the Shakyas before his victorious chariot? To display him as one would a defanged tiger among women and children? It seems to me that such a death in martyrdom would be more desirable to the honorable Commander Vallabha than such a miserable existence. Commander, I, too, have a beloved person in this world still (sobbing, bewildered, to himself) — Oh, what have I said? My mind has revolted! It tries to escape its prison! Reason, you guards, seize it!

    Vikram: That is to say, that is to say! Brave youth, why are you faltering! Be firm and clearly state what your heart feels!

    Sulochana: (Composing himself) Maharaj, forgive me! My speech wavered slightly. You ask what my mind says! But just as kings imprison internal rebels before entering the battlefield, so too has my intellect imprisoned my mind and sealed its mouth, placing reason as a strong guard. Therefore, my mind has no opinion now; I have an opinion, and I, as a soldier of the Shakya nation, am clearly stating it: rather than branding the life, nay, the very memory of the nation with the eternal stigma of cowardly surrender for the sake of one Commander Vallabha’s life, it is fitting that the lives of Vallabha and all of us be sacrificed in fierce combat, utterly destroying the enemy. My conviction is that Vallabha himself is the son of the Patriarch, a brave man by nature, the commander of the Shakyas, and the beloved of a brave woman! I consent to his beheading precisely because if we surrender and Vallabha perchance escapes, that commander himself would not spare us from beheading ourselves for having feared his beheading. Not surrender, but war—that is my final word?

    Vikram: Excellent! Excellent!! O young householder warrior, you have truly made us old ascetic sages bow our heads in shame at your self-control! Indeed, what ascetic could be more self-controlled, more detached, more unblemished than the householder warrior who casts away his own head, as one casually throws a ball of rags, for the sake of his dharma (righteous duty), for his nation, for benevolent justice! Listen, commanders, listen to the final word of this war council! If this young commander had consented to surrender, two votes would have fallen on that side, and that would have become the decisive majority. Come, let our army, which has been fighting continuously for four days, rest until tomorrow morning, and tomorrow, fall upon the enemy as a thunderbolt from the sky. Daily, whilst hewing down the savage hosts of the nation's enemy, Vidyudgarbha, the spurting blood would dye my breast-garments crimson; yet no single stain thereof did fall upon the pristine purity of my heart. But today, today, Nalini, I have come away after the murder of one utterly innocent and blameless. Not the covert or overt slaying of a villain, but the secret murder of a virtuous one, of him who rested his head trustingly upon my lap! The stains of that blood, I feel, have splashed upon my heart! The mere remembrance of that dreadful murder fills me with the dread that some assassin pursues me. Hence, I declare, I must obliterate every trace of this killing. So look, pray look, have any such stains of innocent blood splattered upon my heart! Ah, how can you see my heart merely from without? Peer through my eyes, then shall you perceive my heart. They say the stains of treacherous murder fall upon the heart; and furthermore, my friend, that spilled blood was the blood of one devoted.

When, even in the darkness, I recognised and struck Vallabha with a mortal blow, I then saw her, wounded yet fighting fiercely, in the distant light. Later—

    Anuchar: She must have fallen lifeless there. Among the entire Shakya army, only one Vikramsinh, though himself wounded, was seen roaming the battlefield with some followers, like a blood-lusty tiger, pursuing us with the torch of battle still aflame. The rest of the Shakya army has been cut down; Commander Chand! And our own—

    Chand: The Shakyas fought with such unexpected valour today! Had they fought thus from the outset of the war, we would not have dared to invade them. Nevertheless, I have fulfilled the mission given by His Majesty. The last of the Shakya army has been annihilated. I believe I should take with me another invaluable prize, worthy of offering at the feet of His Majesty Vidudbha, even more than this victory, before proceeding thither. Mark this: if the very trace of the Shakya name is to be erased, then that Buddha must disappear. Be not startled, for even today, he could sow hatred against us in the hearts of thousands from other castes, for Kosala’s treachery against the Shakyas; indeed, he might already be doing so. Therefore, I had intended to slay him in this very ambush; my endeavour to achieve that purpose still does not release me.

    Anuchar: But to escape from here with our lives is itself difficult.

    Chand: If the Shakyas cease fighting, then we shall not transgress the word of Bhagavan (Lord) Buddha and shall make a treaty with the Shakyas; hence, we had previously sent him an alluring invitation for Buddha to come and dissuade the Shakyas from senseless violence. And Buddha, beguiled by this, had agreed to come here this very night. Before dawn, he is still likely to come along this path from the hill to that monastery yonder. Until then, let us lie in wait. If he does not come, we shall escape at daybreak. But if he comes, ah, then! I shall sever his head and crown this Shakya victory! Then, even if we remaining warriors perish in that cause, it matters not. His simple-minded and weak counsel, born of contempt for the force of arms, made the Shakyas simple-minded and proved useful to us. But now, it is expedient not to keep alive him who makes Kosala itself simple-minded. Come! Let us lie hidden by that path beyond these scattered corpses and mounds.

    (They exit.)

    Sulochana: (Entering, blood-stained, hair dishevelled) In this darkness, as far as my sight reaches, I have searched every corpse on this battlefield, but my Vallabha (Beloved), wounded and fallen, is yet nowhere to be seen. When my Nālini (personal name/endearment) perished in battle, from somewhere here rose the roaring challenge of that hero, that Vallabha of mine. My life! Thy task of avenging the Shakyas, as far as possible, is complete. Now it is true that I should let thee go. But my life, for a moment, just for a moment, hold on! Once I have applied the dust from the feet of my Vallabha, fallen on the field of battle, then I shall cast off this body and, my life, consider thee freed.

    (Gazing at the corpses.) Vallabha, Vallabha! There remains no one human to ask for information! Ye gods, deities, spirits, ghosts, forest-deities, war-deities, ye who hear these human questions of my mind, tell me! Where lies the wounded body of the Shakya Commander, Virsinh Vallabha! Vallabha, we were to share the battle-bed together, were we not? Then where art thou alone; I have come to keep thee company, wait a moment, Vallabha! Vallabha—

    Vallabha: (Rising slightly from the battlefield) What is this cry of Sulochana! She had reached the other shore of death, leaving me behind, had she not? Am I then so near to death that I can distinctly hear the call from death's other shore? How swiftly the boat of unconsciousness bears me across the Vaitaraṇī (mythological river of death)!

    Sulochana: Vallabha, art thou anywhere here! Vallabha—

    Vallabha: Oh, my beloved Sulochana! Is this truly thou?

    Sulochana: (Running towards him) Yes, my dear, it is I, my Nath (Lord)! It is I, your Sulochana, who pursued you relentlessly! When you laid me down, dead from your lap, and departed alone for battle, I was so startled that I awoke even from death, and true to my vow, striking and wounding the enemy in your wake, I have returned to your feet. Companion of the bed of love, grant me a place on this battle-bed too, wilt thou not? This is my last plea—

    Vallabha: My life-breaths, draw forth the dwindling flame of my senses but once more. In its flicker, let me see my beloved, feel her, perceive her!

    Sulochana: Ah, my love, how soft and enchanting this battle-bed feels, like that bed of love born of desire and duty!

    Vallabha: For this reason, that bed of love, desirable though it was, was as auspicious and meritorious as this battle-bed of dharma (righteous duty).

    Sulochana: By such a death, our lives have found fulfilment. Our story has reached a happy end.

    Vallabha: Whether any event concludes happily or tragically depends upon what we consider happiness. To fight unto death for the welfare of the people was our wish yesterday; that it has been fulfilled, makes even death a happy end! Ah! Sulochana, art thou near? My life—this is the last call, Sulochana—

    Sulochana: And this is the last, oh! Oh Vallabha!

    Vikram: A sound came from somewhere here. Hmm, come this way, let us see! Ah, this is my brave son, the brave general of the Shakyas, Vallabha, and alas, alas! Nay, thrice blessed! This is my daughter-in-law, Vallabha’s Vallabhā, the war-deity (raṇadevată) of the Shakyas, Sulochana! Bravo, my child! When, in the carnage of this night’s battle, I learned that the young warrior Sulochan in this Shakya army was none other than my daughter-in-law, Sulochana, the joyful wonder I felt seeing thee fight like a descending lightning bolt is now doubled with delight and astonishment to behold thee here, like a lightning bolt held by some magic, embracing thy beloved even on the bed of death, lying lifeless! Oh, the devoted marital duty (naiṣṭhika kāmacharya) of these heroic lovers, male and female, is not one whit less in sanctity than the devoted celibacy (brahmacharya) of an ascetic; in beneficence, it may even be slightly more! That Devi Yashodhara is called a renunciant foremost female disciple (Agraśrāvikā), a great nun (Mahābhikṣuṇī) among the Shakyas, is fitting indeed. But the renunciation, self-control, and devotion to duty of this young maiden, Sulochana, this worshipper of Kāmadeva (god of love), are as intense as those of that great Devi Yashodhara, and her martyrdom (hutātmatā) is truly extraordinary! Oh, ye worshippers of Kāmadeva, what can be more selfless, detached, and unattached than such auspicious, desire-filled devotion to union as yours! My brave, virtuous, beautiful daughter-in-law, child Sulochana, this last embrace given to thy great hero Vallabha on the death-couch, this final desirable sacrament of Kāmadeva's worship for you both, is as auspicious as any religious rite of deity worship; it is holy; it is conducive to the welfare of all young maidens (kanyaka) and youths (kumāra).

And he is wounded, so what are you waiting for? Before the Buddha, crush the throat of this prey (mṛgayā), which has walked into our mouths, with your teeth like a hawk!

    Vikram: O wicked Chand, I too was searching for thee! Take this!

    Buddha Disciple: (Entering with the Buddha) Ha! Ha! Tathāgata Bhagavān Buddha (The Enlightened, Blessed Buddha) counsels both of you to be calm. For he has come to establish peace.

    Chand: Away! Wouldst thou snatch a deer from a lion's mouth?

    (He rushes at Vikram.)

    Buddha: (Supporting Vikram) Vikram, Commander Chand is battle-crazed (raṇonmat). Anger, like an enraged serpent, becomes uncontrollable for anyone. But you are wise. You know our religious aphorism (dharma sūtra): ‘Akrōdhēna jiyēt krōdham | Asādhum sādhunā jiyēt |’ (Conquer anger with non-anger; conquer the evil with good.)

    Chand: What, vile Buddha, dost thou call me evil (asādhu)? Dost thou call the Kosala Commander evil? I shall cut off thy tongue, take this!

    (He advances upon the Buddha.)

    Vikram: (Striking Chand and wounding him, then felling him and planting his foot on his chest) Bhagavān, has his anger been appeased by your non-anger at least? Or has he, conversely, grown more frenzied? If this sword, protector of the good (sādhuparitravāṇa), had not intervened, the righteous Buddha would now have been slain by the wicked (duṣṭa) Chand.

    Buddha: But whatever has happened, such killing does not become you; you are an ascetic (saṁnyāsī)!

    Vikram: And precisely for that reason, the first and most fatal blow to sever the head of this incorrigible public menace (loka kaṇṭak) must be struck by this very renounced sword of mine! It has been struck; I have finally slain this villain (chaṇḍāḷa) myself. This body of mine, weakened by wounds from battle, will fall in half a moment. But it will fall now with even greater fulfilment! Ah! By the spurting splashes of his blood, my ascetic garb and this renounced sword of mine—

    Buddha: Have been stained by a sinful blackness (kāḷibhyāne) that can never be washed away.

    Vikram: Bhagavān, come into the light of reality and look closely, for any with eyes can tell that this is a brilliant red colour, not black. Stained by the red blood of that wicked man, the original sickly, pale yellow of this ascetic's demeanour has vanished, and it appears vibrant, crimson, and radiant like the rising sun! In forty years of unarmed asceticism (niḥśastra saṁnyāsa), I could not accumulate as much beneficence (paropakāra) and merit (puṇya) as I have gathered in this single moment by wielding this ascetic’s weapon, granting life to countless innocent souls. If readiness for public welfare is the worldly duty of an ascetic, then I have never performed it with such intensity as I have today. Therefore, today I truly appear as an ascetic and a monk.

    Buddha: O Mahāmanā (Great-souled one) Vikram, though your logical argument (koṭikram) may seem unassailable from the perspective of worldly gains and losses (aihik lābhālābh), from an otherworldly (pāralaukik) standpoint, you have deviated today from the great vow (mahāvrata) of non-violence (ahiṁsā), in accordance with the ascetic's dharma, which you had taken. Thereby, a desireless (vitṛṣṇa), supremely wise (param jñānī), and foremost renunciant monk (tyāgī bhikṣu pravar) like you, though very near to Nirvāṇa (liberation/enlightenment), has today fallen from Nirvāṇa (Nirvāṇachyut).

    Vikram: If I am denied the state of Nirvāṇa for this fault of slaying this wicked man to avert the violence against countless innocent men and women that would ensue from Chand’s tyranny, then I condemn (dhikkār) such Nirvāṇa. That which is obtained only by aiding the oppression of millions of people is not Nirvāṇa but hell (naraka).

    Dharma: (Manifesting) But O great practitioner of Karma Yoga (Mahākarmayogin), hero, you have not fallen from Nirvāṇa. On the contrary, by this very martyrdom, you have attained the state of Nirvāṇa. For,

    Dvāvimau puruṣau loke Sūryamaṇḍalabhedinau | Parivrāṭ yogayuktaścha raṇe chābhimukhe hataḥ || (Two kinds of men in this world pierce the solar orb: The wandering ascetic (parivrāṭ) devoted to Yoga, And he who is slain in battle, facing forward.)

    Vikram: O divine being, who art thou? A god or an angel (Devdūta)?

    Dharma: I am Dharma, the foundation (dharmādhishṭhān) of all human righteousness. O Buddha-deva, you are supreme among wandering ascetics! Humanity (mānuṣakāvar) owes you infinite gratitude. This Shakya nation and this Shakya race have perished today, but they passed away after fulfilling their chief life’s work. For they gave birth to Tathāgata Buddha! And therefore, in a sense, they are immortal. Gods and demons, yakṣas (nature spirits) and takṣakas (serpent-demons), Āryas (noble ones) and Anāryas (non-noble ones), all of humanity, from islands to continents, shall seek out this your birthplace as the most sacred land, to offer their floral oblations of devotion (bhaktibhāvāchyā puṣpāñjali) here. The histories of this Shakya race shall be studied like religious epics (dharmapurāṇā sārakhe); kings and emperors shall cast their crowns, and the seven seas their rubies and pearls, before your image. Millions upon millions of human heads, which have not bowed before any man’s feet, shall humbly bow before the feet of your meditative image for ages to come. O wandering ascetic, great karma-renunciant Buddha, you are indeed worthy of the state of Nirvāṇa; but O hero, great practitioner of Karma Yoga Vikram, you too are worthy of that same state of Nirvāṇa! For, to achieve the ultimate good of humanity, which is the chief essence of Dharma, you renounced everything and fought for public welfare (lokakalyāṇārth) in direct combat, slaying those who harm the people! You did not even flinch from sacrificing your own life (prāṇatyāg). Unfortunately, your memory as an individual will not endure like the Buddha’s. But fame is often a matter of chance; it is not the sole indicator of merit. And the terrible penalty that this Shakya race has had to pay for this oblivion of your Karma Yoga’s greatness, how this feeble Shakya nation fell prey to the violence of powerful villains due to its neglect of armed strength and the extreme asceticism of agriculture, women, and the sword (kṛṣi, kāminī, kṛpāṇ), so too, and for almost the same reasons, will this entire Āryāvarta (ancient India) fall prey to the demonic invasions of Yavanas, Huns, and Shakas (yavana-huṇa-śakāṁchyā rākṣasī ākramaṇāsa). This destruction of the Shakya nation today is merely a premonition of the grave danger that will befall the entire Āryāvarta in the future. It is merely a preliminary experiment conducted by Time on a small scale. Hero Vikram, adopting the auspicious, power-worshipping Karma Yoga (śubha kāmī śaktipūjak karmayoga) that you preached and practiced, which contains all the worldly and otherworldly benefits of inactive asceticism yet is supremely beneficial for the people, is the means to liberation from the clutches of that danger. Since the true nature and objective of Dharma, which is the worldly and otherworldly sustenance and salvation of humanity, is achieved to a greater extent through such Karma Yoga, O humanity, my definite and best opinion is that although—

    Saṁnyāsaḥ karmayogaścha niḥśreyasakārāvubhau || Tathāpi -- tayostu karmasaṁnyāsāt karmayogo viśiṣyate || (Renunciation and the path of action both lead to supreme good. Yet, of the two, the path of action is superior to the renunciation of action.)
